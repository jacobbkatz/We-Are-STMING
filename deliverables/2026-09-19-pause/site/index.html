We built a scanning tunneling microscope in a basement

We detected tunneling. Electrons crossed a gap that nothing was touching, and an amplifier we built measured them. What we could not do was hold that gap still long enough to take a picture.

A scanning tunneling microscope reads a surface by measuring a current a billionth of an amp flowing across a gap that nothing is touching. We built one over a summer on a wooden bench on a concrete floor, between our first and second years of university. No laboratory, no faculty advisor, no grant, and neither of us had written a program or soldered anything that had to work. Every number here can be recomputed from the raw files, and what each part of the instrument actually did is set out one row at a time.

What a commercial instrument buys is not only the hardware: it is the vacuum, the cooling and the isolation that make the gap hold still — which is exactly what stopped us.

Did it work?

A scanning tunneling microscope is a dozen machines that all have to work at once. Ours is twelve, and the answer is different for each one.

Command and firmware
Works
Eighteen four-character commands over USB, driving four 16-bit converters, a 16-bit detector and a stepper motor from one microcontroller. Two faults that would have crashed a tip were found by reading the firmware before a tip was ever fitted — both inherited from upstream — then fixed and bench-tested: engaging the feedback loop now leaves the scanner where it was, where the old code slammed it to midscale.
Bias on the sample
Works
A commanded zero puts +3.000 V on the sample, and the chain returned −9,586 counts against the −9,600 that voltage predicts.
The preamplifier
Works
Boxed, on its screws, at ±15 V, it invents 4 picoamps of current of its own — a tunneling current is 250 times that. Getting there meant finding that the boards we had made came back with no ground pour on them, so the amplifier’s non-inverting input was floating on both copies.
The measurement chain, end to end
Works
Tip holder to a number on the screen, measured against a resistor whose value we already knew: 0.13 of one standard error from what Ohm’s law requires. The measurement is below.
Fine positioning in Z
No distance scale
Stepping the piezo by ±2,000 counts moved the current by 6,305 ± 1,484 counts — 4.2 standard errors from nothing, so the scanner demonstrably controls the gap. How far 2,000 counts is, in nanometers, we have never measured on our own hardware.
Fine positioning in X and Y
No distance scale
The scanned surface tilts 0.105 Z counts for every X count, so the 30,000-count jump back at the start of each pass leaves 3,144 counts of height for the loop to recover; about 2,470 showed up. The same arithmetic predicts the effect should be absent from the runs that do not fly back that way, and in both of those it was. Its size in nanometers is unmeasured too.
The feedback loop
Closes, never held
It settles rather than running away, and holds the current to about 0.06 of a decade at 2,000 pixels per second, with 5, 15 and 250 corrections per pixel all agreeing. Every image tuned for the 19 September junction still aborted at a clamp.
Coarse approach
Finds the surface, cannot stop
The motor found the sample twice, after 200 and 180 steps in chunks of twenty. Both times it went into contact within thirty seconds. It cannot stop inside the window where a tunneling current is visible: single steps were seen to shift the surface by 14,000 to 29,000 scanner counts when they moved it at all, while runs of twenty to forty steps did nothing. That is what the piezo is for; the handover between them never worked.
The sample
Real gold, badly mounted
It is gold — flame-tested on a 2 mm scrap. Directly underneath it sit fifty to a hundred micrometers of backing paper, and the plate is held down by twisted rubber bands.
The tip
Never sharpened
Every tip ever fitted was a blunt cut placeholder, and one turned out to be bent and touching with its side. We built a setup for etching sharp tungsten tips; our own inventory records it as unknown whether it was ever used successfully, or whether any tip off it reached the instrument.
Vibration isolation
Never tested
Three springs loaded with coin mass, and eddy damping from magnets over an aluminum plate. The only measurement we took was with the tip clear of the surface, which tests electrical pickup and not mechanical isolation. The suspension’s resonant frequency is unknown. It is also not what stopped us — the motion that did is far too slow to be vibration.
A repeatable image
No
Our scans repeat at +0.04. The control runs, which cannot contain a surface at all, repeat at +0.07. Indistinguishable, and neither differs from zero.

Nothing in the first four rows is in our way. What is in our way is in the last three, and all of it is mechanical. That is worth knowing precisely: it means the electronics we designed, built and brought up are done, and the work left is a rigid sample, a sharp tip, and finding out what moves the gap.

What a scanning tunneling microscope is

An ordinary microscope cannot show you an atom. It works with light, and light comes in waves about 500 nanometers long. An atom is a quarter of one nanometer. The ruler is two thousand times bigger than the thing you are trying to measure.

So an STM throws the ruler away. It sharpens a metal needle until it ends in a single atom. It brings that needle to within about a nanometer of the surface. Then it puts a small voltage across the gap and measures the current.

The part that sounds impossible

Nothing is touching. In everyday physics that means no current: water does not cross a gap in a pipe. Quantum mechanics does not work like that.

An electron is not a little ball sitting in one place. It is spread out. A small part of it is already on the far side of the gap. So there is a real chance it simply appears over there. That is tunneling.

The current it makes is about one nanoamp. That is a billionth of an amp — roughly a billion times smaller than what runs through a phone charger.

Here is the useful part. Move the tip a tenth of a nanometer closer — less than half the width of an atom — and the current goes up about tenfold.

So do this. Slide the tip sideways across the surface. As you go, keep adjusting its height so the current never changes. The tip is now following every bump it passes over. Write down the height as you go and you have drawn the surface, atom by atom. That is the picture an STM makes.

What we built

Ours is about a foot tall, made of printed plastic, threaded rod and copper tape, and it does every one of the things on the previous page.

We designed it, ordered the boards, printed the frame, wrote the firmware, and brought up every stage on its own before trusting the next. The mechanics, controller and firmware follow Mech Panda’s open red-panda-stm; the scan head and preamplifier follow Dan Berard’s home-built STM. The electronics bring-up, the software, the calibration and the controls are ours.

What we measured

Before believing anything the instrument said about a surface, we made it measure something whose answer we already knew.

0.13 σ
How close we came to theory
We took the tip and the sample out and clipped in a resistor whose value we already knew. Ohm’s law says exactly what the instrument should then report. Over 53 readings ours differed from that by 0.13 of one standard error — about a tenth of what our own scatter would explain. Indistinguishable from exactly right.
4 pA
How much the amplifier invents
Every amplifier makes up a little current of its own, and that sets the smallest real signal it can find. Ours makes up 4 picoamps. A tunneling current is about a nanoamp — 250 times bigger. The amplifier is not what is in our way.
16–59 MΩ
What the junction looked like
Resistance is how hard it is to push current through something. A wire is almost zero. A gap is almost infinite. Ours sat between the two — and it fell as we raised the voltage. A wire never does that. A barrier does.
9–19
How quiet it was
With a real junction live, the reading wobbled by only 9 to 19 counts. The signal we are hunting is thousands of counts. So the electronics are not our limit. What stopped us is mechanical, and that is the next section.

How we know electrons tunneled

The argument is four steps long and every one of them is either measured here or standard physics. It is also the claim on this page we have argued about most.

  1. The tip went from far away to touching. At the start there is no measurable current; at the end the amplifier is saturated. Both ends are measured, on every approach.
  2. So it passed through every separation in between, including the range from about two nanometers down to a few tenths of one. A tip does not teleport.
  3. In that range, tunneling is the only mechanism left. At half a volt across a nanometer the field is about 5×108 volts per meter — too low for field emission — and at room temperature electrons cannot climb over a metal’s work function either. What is left is electrons going through.
  4. The bias was on and the amplifier was recording the whole way. Across 109 separate approaches we took 60,928 readings inside exactly that range.

There is one way out of that argument: a metal bridge could form before the tip ever gets close and carry the current instead, and then nothing had to tunnel. Four measurements of our own close it:

  • The current climbs far faster than the voltage. Over a tenfold voltage range it rose thirty-seven fold. A metal contact is ohmic — it would have risen tenfold.
  • It is symmetric in both bias directions to about ten percent, measured as eight rounds with the polarity interleaved so that drift could not manufacture the slope.
  • The junction never fell below about 5 megohms, even with the amplifier saturated. A metal contact is far lower; a single-atom point contact would be around 13 kilohms, and ours ran about a thousand times higher than that.
  • 109 of 110 approaches came on as a gradual ramp. A bridge snapping shut makes a step, and we recorded exactly one of those.

What this does not say

It does not say we held a clean vacuum gap. Two things in our own numbers point away from that. 16 to 59 megohms is at the low end for STM tunneling, which usually runs from about 100 megohms up — nearer contact than a textbook junction. And the current rises as roughly the 1.55 power of the voltage, where an ideal vacuum gap at these biases should be closer to ohmic. Both are consistent with a barrier that is not clean vacuum — a contaminant film or a thin oxide. Electrons still have to tunnel through one of those. You just cannot command its width, which is what an image needs.

So the honest sentence has two halves, and only the second one fails. Electrons tunneled, and our amplifier measured them.

What we never did was hold that gap steady. To make a picture you have to keep it open, at the right width, long enough to drag the tip across a surface. That is the part we could not do.

What stopped us

Ranked by how much each one actually blocked an image, not by how much time went into it. None of the five is electronic.

1

The gap will not hold still

With the motor stopped and nobody touching the instrument, the surface moves by more than 43,000 converter counts in 6.4 seconds — most of the piezo’s entire range. An image needs it inside a few hundred counts for a minute. This is the blocker, and the motion is far too slow to be vibration, so a better suspension is the wrong fix.

Possible fix. Put a box over it and record for fifteen minutes before touching anything. The cause is unknown among seven candidates, and the two cheapest to eliminate cost nothing to test: room air, and two unclamped cables running from the suspended platform to the fixed bench.
2

The sample is soft

Gold leaf resting on its backing paper — fifty to a hundred micrometers of compressible cellulose directly under the surface we were trying to hold still to a fraction of a nanometer — with the plate held on by twisted rubber bands. It moved when Jacob blew on it.

Possible fix. Bond the gold to a rigid substrate with no paper under it, and clamp the plate mechanically instead of with rubber bands.
3

Every tip was a placeholder

A tunneling current should come from a single atom. Ours came from blunt tips, and one of them turned out to be bent and touching with its side. A blunt tip presents many atoms at once, which is exactly what turns a gap into a pressed contact.

Possible fix. Etch a sharp tip with the setup we already own — and record the result, which our inventory says nobody has.
4

No distance scale of our own

We never established how far the tip moves per converter count on our own hardware. The consequence is the sharpest thing on this list: for a while scans were covering about ±0.6 nanometers of sample — smaller than a single atom, far too small to show a surface, and nobody could have known. That figure rests on a scale this instrument has never established, which is the row you are reading.

Possible fix. Measure one distance inside the scan head under a jeweler’s loupe. Minutes, no power, and it fixes the nanometer scale for every measurement this instrument will ever make.
5

The approach cannot park in the window

Single motor steps were seen to shift the surface by 14,000 to 29,000 scanner counts when they moved it at all, while runs of twenty to forty steps did nothing at all. The window where a tunneling current is visible is a few hundred counts wide. The motor cannot stop inside it — which is not a fault, it is why the piezo exists — but the handover from one to the other never worked.

Possible fix. Settle the sign of the tunneling current so the automatic approach is safe to run, then hand the last stage from the motor to the piezo.

This is the row we would most like help with. If you have chased mechanical drift at this scale and found it, we would like to know how. Write to us.

How we knew when we were fooling ourselves

After every scan we ran the same scan again with the sideways sweep switched off.

That control has identical timing and identical feedback behavior, but the tip never travels across the surface, so it cannot contain surface structure at all. Anything appearing in both is the instrument, not the sample. Our scans repeated no better than those controls did, and that single test is what settles the imaging question.

Two of the numbers we first published about this comparison turned out to have been computed across files that stopped early. We withdrew both, and the conclusion held on the rest.

The second instrument

Every line of code here was written by Claude — the firmware, the analysis, the figures, the documents. Every measurement in it was taken by hand, at a bench, by us. The interesting part is neither of those. It is what had to be built in between, so that the two could be trusted together.

That arrangement has an obvious problem. A model will write you a confident paragraph about a measurement it half-remembers, and it reads exactly like the paragraphs that are right. Here a wrong number costs a piezo or a week.

But the thing that actually kills a project like this is duller. It is a number corrected in one document and left standing in the eleven others that quote it. One day two of our constants changed. Both were already written into more than a dozen files. We corrected every copy anyone could think of — and stale values were still sitting in six of them.

So we built an operating system for the project

Not a model. The model is off the shelf. What is ours is everything around it.

A set of rules it has to read before it does anything. One file that owns every number. A log for each working session that is never rewritten. And a program that checks the whole lot and refuses the commit if anything is wrong.

The protocol

CLAUDE.md

575 lines the model reads before anything else: how to start a session, which document wins when two disagree, and the hardware rules that cost money to break. It is written in plain language because it is also how we tell it what we have learned.

The register

docs/FACTS.md

One canonical value for every number that matters, each with its units, how it is known — measured, calculated, read off a datasheet, or simply told to us — and the date. Change a value here first, and every stale copy elsewhere becomes findable.

The checker

Code/pc/check_facts.py

598 lines, seven classes of test. It hunts retired values sitting in live documents, broken file references, superseded procedures being cited as current, and safety-rule citations that no longer point at the rule they name. It runs at every session start and a Git hook blocks the commit when it fails.

The logbook

sessions/

One append-only log per working session, 28 of them, each with what was measured, what was ruled out and what went wrong. Past readings are never edited. When a later session corrects an earlier one, the correction is written beside it, not over it.

The shared spine

git · GitHub

Two people, two computers, often days apart. Every session starts by pulling and ends by pushing, and a start-up hook does the pull, runs the checker and prints what changed since last time. 264 commits. Neither of us has ever had to ask the other what state the project was in.

The review pass

LEAD_VERIFICATION.md

Every claim re-derived independently from the raw files before it was allowed into anything public — including three where the reviewer was wrong and had to be corrected back. That document is on this page, unedited.

What proves it works is the retractions

Anyone can get an AI to write confident prose. The test is whether it will take something back. This machinery made the project withdraw four things it had already published:

  • A statistic computed across files that stopped early.
  • A feature that kept reappearing in our scans. It turned out to be the feedback loop recovering from a jump, not the sample.
  • A detector’s full-scale voltage, written down backwards in a live reference.
  • Eleven wrong numbers in a single commit, caught before the second push.

Every one of those is still in the record beside what replaced it. None of them was caught by either of us reading carefully. They were caught by a rule that said where a number lives, and a program that checked.

What we had not seen done is a real instrument run this way: the writing and the arithmetic delegated, every measurement still taken by hand, and a framework in between strict enough to force retractions. Why we built it

An STM is one of the few instruments that can resolve a single atom. It is also one of the very few of those that an amateur can build.

That is only true because two people published theirs for free: Mech Panda’s red-panda-stm, and Dan Berard’s home-built STM. We had their designs, a 3D printer, a soldering iron and a basement.

So the first weeks were not measurement. They were learning what the parts even were: what an amplifier like ours does, why the tip has to end in one atom, and why a chip that reads a voltage cannot simply be pointed at a current.

Some of it had to be made rather than bought. Tungsten tips are etched, not machined, so we put together a setup for it: a bench supply, sodium hydroxide, and a wire lowered into it, outdoors and in gloves, because the chemistry is not gentle.

What is unusual about this project is not the physics, which is forty years old. It is that everything that did not work is written down beside everything that did.

  • Self-directed. No faculty advisor, no grant, no laboratory.
  • Eleven weeks of planning and design, and eight weeks of building. Where one ended and the other began, we never wrote down.
  • Twenty-eight working sessions, each one logged and never rewritten.
  • 163 raw data files and 92 photographs of our own hardware, all public.

Where it goes next

The instrument is in pieces, moved between two houses at the end of the summer. When it goes back together, the first thing is a measurement that needs no power and takes minutes: how far the tip sits from the line through the two ball supports. It sets the nanometer scale for everything the instrument will ever measure, and it settles our best junction retroactively.

Then the free test for what moves the gap — a box over the instrument and a fifteen-minute recording — then a rigid sample and a sharp tip. None of the five things in our way needs a purchase, and none needs a laboratory.

Tell us what is moving the gap

We are two undergraduates who wanted to see single atoms and got most of the way to the instrument that could show us one. It is in pieces right now, in a different house from where it was built, and we are both back at university.

The part we would most like help with is not money or parts. It is the first row of what stopped us: something on that bench moves the gap by most of the scanner’s range within seconds, and we have seven candidates and no way to rank them. If you have ever chased mechanical drift at this scale, we would like to hear how you found it.
