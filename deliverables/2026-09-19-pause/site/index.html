What the instrument is

A scanning tunnelling microscope reads a surface by measuring a current that flows between a sharp metal tip and the sample without the two ever touching. The current is about a billionth of an amp, and it changes tenfold for every tenth of a nanometre the gap changes.

That sensitivity is the whole instrument and the whole problem. It is what lets an STM resolve single atoms, and it is why the gap has to be held still to a fraction of the width of one.

We designed it, ordered the boards, printed the frame, wrote the firmware, and brought up every stage on its own before trusting the next. The mechanics, controller and firmware follow Mech Panda’s open red-panda-stm; the scan head and preamplifier follow Dan Berard’s home-built STM. The electronics bring-up, the software, the calibration and the controls are ours.

Did we detect tunnelling?

Yes. The argument is short, and every step of it is measured rather than assumed.

A tip cannot travel from not-touching to touching without passing through the separations where quantum tunnelling is the only thing that carries electrons across. We had a voltage applied and the amplifier recording the entire way. Across 109 separate approaches we took 60,928 readings inside exactly that range.

The only way out of that argument would be a metal-to-metal bridge forming before the tip ever got close — and our own data closes it. The current climbs faster than the voltage, the junction never fell below about 5 megohms even with the amplifier saturated, and 109 of 110 approaches came on as a gradual ramp rather than the step a bridge would make.

So the honest sentence has two halves and only the second one fails. Electrons tunnelled, and our amplifier measured them. What we have not done is hold that gap open and steady enough to drag a tip across a surface and build a picture — and that is the thing an STM has to do.

What stopped us

Ranked by how much each one actually blocked an image, not by how much time went into it. None of the five is electronic.

1

The gap will not hold still

With the motor stopped and nobody touching the instrument, the surface moves by more than 43,000 converter counts in 6.4 seconds — most of the piezo’s entire range. An image needs it inside a few hundred counts for a minute. This is the blocker, and the motion is far too slow to be vibration, so a better suspension is the wrong fix.

Possible fix. Put a box over it and record for fifteen minutes before touching anything. The cause is unknown among seven candidates, and the two cheapest to eliminate cost nothing to test: room air, and two unclamped cables running from the suspended platform to the fixed bench.
2

The sample is soft

Gold leaf resting on its backing paper — fifty to a hundred micrometres of compressible cellulose directly under the surface we were trying to hold still to a fraction of a nanometre — with the plate held on by two twisted rubber bands. It moved when Jacob blew on it.

Possible fix. Bond the gold to a rigid substrate with no paper under it, and clamp the plate mechanically instead of with rubber bands.
3

Every tip was a placeholder

A tunnelling current should come from a single atom. Ours came from blunt tips, and one of them turned out to be bent and touching with its side. A blunt tip presents many atoms at once, which is exactly what turns a gap into a pressed contact.

Possible fix. Etch a sharp tip with the setup we already own and have already used.
4

No distance scale of our own

We never established how far the tip moves per converter count on our own hardware. The consequence is the sharpest thing on this list: for a while every scan covered about 0.6 nanometres of sample — smaller than a single atom — and nobody could have known.

Possible fix. Measure one distance inside the scan head under a jeweller’s loupe. Minutes, no power, and it fixes the nanometre scale for every measurement this instrument will ever make.
5

The approach cannot park in the window

One step of the coarse motor moves the tip at least eleven times the width of the window where tunnelling is visible. There is no motor position inside it. That is not a fault, it is why the piezo exists — but the handover from one to the other never worked.

Possible fix. Settle the sign of the tunnelling current so the automatic approach is safe to run, then hand the last stage from the motor to the piezo.

This is the row we would most like help with. If you have chased mechanical drift at this scale and found it, we would like to know how. Write to us.

How we knew when we were fooling ourselves

After every scan we ran the same scan again with the sideways sweep switched off.

That control has identical timing and identical feedback behaviour, but the tip never travels across the surface, so it cannot contain surface structure at all. Anything appearing in both is the instrument, not the sample. Our scans repeated no better than those controls did, and that single test is what settles the imaging question.

We went back over our own published numbers at the end and found two of them had been computed across files that stopped early. We withdrew both. The conclusion survived on other evidence, and the withdrawn claims are still in the record beside what replaced them.

The second instrument

Neither of us writes code. So the other thing we built was the framework that does, and that catches it when it is wrong.

Two people, two computers, days apart. The failure that kills a project like this is not a hard experiment — it is a number corrected in one document and left standing in the eleven others that quote it. That is not hypothetical: one day two constants changed, both were already written into more than a dozen documents, and correcting every copy anyone could think of still left stale values in six files.

So every number in the project now lives in exactly one place, carries a tag recording how it is known, and is checked by a program rather than by memory.

575

164

What proves it works is the retractions. The checks and verification passes forced this project to withdraw its own published claims — a statistical result computed across truncated files, a reproducible feature that turned out to be the feedback loop rather than the sample, a detector’s full-scale voltage recorded backwards in a live reference, and eleven wrong numbers in a single commit caught before the second push.

An AI that writes confident prose is not hard to get. One that produces a retraction is, and that is the part we built. We did not train a model; the model is off the shelf. The discipline around it is ours.

About us

We are Jacob Katz and Nuh Shaheer, first-year students at the University of California San Diego and Worcester Polytechnic Institute. We built this in a basement over the summer, because we wanted to see single atoms.

Why we built it

A scanning tunnelling microscope is one of the few instruments that resolves single atoms, and one of the very few of those that an amateur can build — because two people published theirs for nothing: Mech Panda’s red-panda-stm and Dan Berard’s home-built STM. We had their designs, a 3D printer, a soldering iron and a basement.

Neither of us had written a program. Neither of us had really soldered — this was the first time either of us had picked up an iron for something that had to work. So the first weeks were not measurement, they were learning what the parts were: what a transimpedance amplifier does, why the tip has to be sharp to a single atom, why a converter that reads a voltage cannot simply be pointed at a current.

Some of it had to be made rather than bought. Tungsten tips are etched, not machined, so we built an etcher: a bench supply, sodium hydroxide, and a wire lowered into it, run outdoors in gloves because the chemistry is not gentle.

What we do differently

Nothing about the physics. What is unusual is the bookkeeping. Every number in the project lives in exactly one file, carries a tag saying whether it was measured, calculated, read off a datasheet or simply told to us, and is checked by a program at the start of every working session. Every scan was run again with the sideways sweep switched off, so we always had a result that could not contain a surface to compare against. Twice that machinery made us withdraw something we had already published.

We are not claiming an image. We are claiming a measurement chain that agrees with theory, a junction that behaves like a barrier, and a limit we found and measured rather than guessed at. Everything that did not work is written down beside everything that did.

What we are building toward

An image of a surface, taken by an instrument we built, at a scale where the atoms are visible. We are not there. None of the five things standing in the way needs a purchase and none needs a laboratory — which is the reason this is a pause and not an ending.

Where it goes next

The instrument is in pieces, moved between two houses while one of us started college. When it goes back together, the first thing is a measurement that needs no power and takes minutes: how far the tip sits from the line through the two ball supports. It sets the nanometre scale for everything the instrument will ever measure, and it settles our best junction retroactively.

Then the free test for what moves the gap — a box over the instrument and a fifteen-minute recording — then a rigid sample and a sharp tip. None of the five things in our way needs a purchase, and none needs a laboratory.

Tell us what is moving the gap

We are two first-year students who wanted to see single atoms and got most of the way to the instrument that can. It is in pieces right now, in a different house from where it was built, while one of us starts college.

The part we would most like help with is not money or parts. It is the first row of what stopped us: something on that bench moves the gap by most of the scanner’s range within seconds, and we have seven candidates and no way to rank them. If you have ever chased mechanical drift at this scale, we would like to hear how you found it.

Corrections are just as welcome. If a number on this page is wrong, tell us and we will change it and say who caught it.
