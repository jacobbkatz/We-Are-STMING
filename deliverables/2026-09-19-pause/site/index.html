We built a scanning tunnelling microscope in a basement

We detected tunnelling — electrons crossing a gap narrower than an atom, measured by an amplifier we built.

A scanning tunnelling microscope reads a surface by measuring a current a billionth of an amp flowing across a gap that nothing is touching. We built one over a summer on a wooden bench on a concrete floor, between our first and second years of university. No laboratory, no faculty advisor, no grant. Neither of us had written a program or really soldered before this. Every measurement on this page can be recomputed from the raw files, and what we could not do is set out as plainly as what we could.

What a scanning tunnelling microscope is

If you have never met one: it is a microscope that does not use light, and it never touches the thing it is looking at.

An optical microscope cannot show you an atom. Light comes in waves about 500 nanometers long and an atom is about a quarter of one nanometer across — two thousand times smaller than the ruler. So a scanning tunnelling microscope throws the ruler away. It brings a metal needle, sharpened until it ends in a single atom, to within about a nanometer of a surface, puts a small voltage across the gap, and measures the current that flows across it.

The part that sounds impossible

Nothing is touching. In everyday physics no current can flow across a gap, the way water will not cross a gap in a pipe. Quantum mechanics does not work like that. An electron is not a little ball with a definite position; it is spread out, and a small part of it is already on the far side of the gap. So there is a real chance the electron simply appears over there. That is tunnelling, and the current it makes is about one nanoamp — a billionth of an amp, roughly a billion times smaller than the current in a phone charger.

The useful part is how violently that current changes with distance. Move the tip a tenth of a nanometer closer — less than half an atom — and the current goes up about tenfold. Which means that if you slide the tip sideways across a surface and continually adjust its height to keep the current the same, the path it traces is the surface itself, bump by bump, atom by atom. That is the picture an STM makes.

What one normally costs

This is not usually a thing people build. A commercial instrument is quoted at roughly:

$1,000,000+

$64

The three commercial figures come from a laboratory-equipment supplier’s published breakdown, read in September 2026 — excedr.com. They are the only numbers on this page we did not measure ourselves, and they are here for scale, not as a claim about what ours is worth. What a commercial instrument buys is not only the hardware: it is the vacuum, the cooling and the isolation that make the gap hold still — which is exactly what stopped us.

What we built

Ours is about a foot tall, made of printed plastic, threaded rod and copper tape, and it does every one of the things on the previous page.

We designed it, ordered the boards, printed the frame, wrote the firmware, and brought up every stage on its own before trusting the next. The mechanics, controller and firmware follow Mech Panda’s open red-panda-stm; the scan head and preamplifier follow Dan Berard’s home-built STM. The electronics bring-up, the software, the calibration and the controls are ours.

Did we detect tunnelling?

Yes. The argument is short, and every step of it is measured rather than assumed.

A tip cannot travel from not-touching to touching without passing through the separations where quantum tunnelling is the only thing that carries electrons across. We had a voltage applied and the amplifier recording the entire way. Across 109 separate approaches we took 60,928 readings inside exactly that range.

The only way out of that argument would be a metal-to-metal bridge forming before the tip ever got close — and our own data closes it. The current climbs faster than the voltage, the junction never fell below about 5 megohms even with the amplifier saturated, and 109 of 110 approaches came on as a gradual ramp rather than the step a bridge would make.

So the honest sentence has two halves and only the second one fails. Electrons tunnelled, and our amplifier measured them. What we have not done is hold that gap open and steady enough to drag a tip across a surface and build a picture — and that is the thing an STM has to do.

What stopped us

Ranked by how much each one actually blocked an image, not by how much time went into it. None of the five is electronic.

1

The gap will not hold still

With the motor stopped and nobody touching the instrument, the surface moves by more than 43,000 converter counts in 6.4 seconds — most of the piezo’s entire range. An image needs it inside a few hundred counts for a minute. This is the blocker, and the motion is far too slow to be vibration, so a better suspension is the wrong fix.

Possible fix. Put a box over it and record for fifteen minutes before touching anything. The cause is unknown among seven candidates, and the two cheapest to eliminate cost nothing to test: room air, and two unclamped cables running from the suspended platform to the fixed bench.
2

The sample is soft

Gold leaf resting on its backing paper — fifty to a hundred micrometers of compressible cellulose directly under the surface we were trying to hold still to a fraction of a nanometer — with the plate held on by two twisted rubber bands. It moved when Jacob blew on it.

Possible fix. Bond the gold to a rigid substrate with no paper under it, and clamp the plate mechanically instead of with rubber bands.
3

Every tip was a placeholder

A tunnelling current should come from a single atom. Ours came from blunt tips, and one of them turned out to be bent and touching with its side. A blunt tip presents many atoms at once, which is exactly what turns a gap into a pressed contact.

Possible fix. Etch a sharp tip with the setup we already own and have already used.
4

No distance scale of our own

We never established how far the tip moves per converter count on our own hardware. The consequence is the sharpest thing on this list: for a while every scan covered about 0.6 nanometers of sample — smaller than a single atom — and nobody could have known.

Possible fix. Measure one distance inside the scan head under a jeweller’s loupe. Minutes, no power, and it fixes the nanometer scale for every measurement this instrument will ever make.
5

The approach cannot park in the window

One step of the coarse motor moves the tip at least eleven times the width of the window where tunnelling is visible. There is no motor position inside it. That is not a fault, it is why the piezo exists — but the handover from one to the other never worked.

Possible fix. Settle the sign of the tunnelling current so the automatic approach is safe to run, then hand the last stage from the motor to the piezo.

This is the row we would most like help with. If you have chased mechanical drift at this scale and found it, we would like to know how. Write to us.

How we knew when we were fooling ourselves

After every scan we ran the same scan again with the sideways sweep switched off.

That control has identical timing and identical feedback behavior, but the tip never travels across the surface, so it cannot contain surface structure at all. Anything appearing in both is the instrument, not the sample. Our scans repeated no better than those controls did, and that single test is what settles the imaging question.

We went back over our own published numbers at the end and found two of them had been computed across files that stopped early. We withdrew both. The conclusion survived on other evidence, and the withdrawn claims are still in the record beside what replaced them.

The second instrument

Neither of us writes code. Claude wrote all of it — the firmware, the analysis, the figures, the documents — and GitHub is what let two people on two computers run that without losing track of what was true.

That arrangement has an obvious failure mode. A model will write you a confident paragraph about a measurement it has half-remembered, and it will do it in a voice that sounds exactly like the paragraphs that are right. On a project where a wrong number can destroy a piezo or waste a week, that is not a small problem.

The failure that actually kills a project like this is duller than a hard experiment: a number corrected in one document and left standing in the eleven others that quote it. That is not hypothetical. One day two constants changed, both were already written into more than a dozen documents, and correcting every copy anyone could think of still left stale values in six files.

So we built an operating system for the project

Not a model — the model is off the shelf. What is ours is everything around it: a set of rules the model has to read before it is allowed to do anything, one file that owns every number, a log per working session that is never rewritten, and a program that checks the whole thing and refuses the commit if it fails.

The protocol

CLAUDE.md

575 lines the model reads before anything else: how to start a session, which document wins when two disagree, and the hardware rules that cost money to break. It is written in plain language because it is also how we tell it what we have learned.

The register

docs/FACTS.md

One canonical value for every number that matters, each with its units, how it is known — measured, calculated, read off a datasheet, or simply told to us — and the date. Change a value here first, and every stale copy elsewhere becomes findable.

The checker

Code/pc/check_facts.py

565 lines, seven classes of test. It hunts retired values sitting in live documents, broken file references, superseded procedures being cited as current, and safety-rule citations that no longer point at the rule they name. It runs at every session start and a Git hook blocks the commit when it fails.

The logbook

sessions/

One append-only log per working session, 27 of them, each with what was measured, what was ruled out and what went wrong. Past readings are never edited. When a later session corrects an earlier one, the correction is written beside it, not over it.

The shared spine

git · GitHub

Two people, two computers, often days apart. Every session starts by pulling and ends by pushing, and a start-up hook does the pull, runs the checker and prints what changed since last time. 106 commits. Neither of us has ever had to ask the other what state the project was in.

The review pass

LEAD_VERIFICATION.md

Every claim re-derived independently from the raw files before it was allowed into anything public — including three where the reviewer was wrong and had to be corrected back. That document is on this page, unedited.

What proves it works is the retractions

Anyone can get an AI to write confident prose. The test is whether it will take something back. This machinery forced the project to withdraw claims it had already published: a statistical result computed across files that stopped early; a reproducible feature in a scan that turned out to be the feedback loop recovering from a jump rather than the sample; a detector’s full-scale voltage recorded backwards in a live reference; and eleven wrong numbers in a single commit, caught before the second push.

Every one of those is still in the record beside what replaced it. None of them was caught by either of us reading carefully. They were caught by a rule that said where a number lives, and a program that checked.

The whole framework is in the repository and is not specific to microscopes.

Why we built it

A scanning tunnelling microscope is one of the few instruments that resolves single atoms, and one of the very few of those that an amateur can build — because two people published theirs for nothing: Mech Panda’s red-panda-stm and Dan Berard’s home-built STM. We had their designs, a 3D printer, a soldering iron and a basement.

Neither of us had written a program. Neither of us had really soldered — this was the first time either of us had picked up an iron for something that had to work. So the first weeks were not measurement, they were learning what the parts were: what a transimpedance amplifier does, why the tip has to be sharp to a single atom, why a converter that reads a voltage cannot simply be pointed at a current.

Some of it had to be made rather than bought. Tungsten tips are etched, not machined, so we built an etcher: a bench supply, sodium hydroxide, and a wire lowered into it, run outdoors in gloves because the chemistry is not gentle.

What we do differently

Nothing about the physics. What is unusual is the bookkeeping. Every number in the project lives in exactly one file, carries a tag saying whether it was measured, calculated, read off a datasheet or simply told to us, and is checked by a program at the start of every working session. Every scan was run again with the sideways sweep switched off, so we always had a result that could not contain a surface to compare against. Twice that machinery made us withdraw something we had already published.

We are not claiming an image. We are claiming a measurement chain that agrees with theory, a junction that behaves like a barrier, and a limit we found and measured rather than guessed at. Everything that did not work is written down beside everything that did.

What we are building toward

An image of a surface, taken by an instrument we built, at a scale where the atoms are visible. We are not there. None of the five things standing in the way needs a purchase and none needs a laboratory — which is the reason this is a pause and not an ending.

Where it goes next

The instrument is in pieces, moved between two houses at the end of the summer. When it goes back together, the first thing is a measurement that needs no power and takes minutes: how far the tip sits from the line through the two ball supports. It sets the nanometer scale for everything the instrument will ever measure, and it settles our best junction retroactively.

Then the free test for what moves the gap — a box over the instrument and a fifteen-minute recording — then a rigid sample and a sharp tip. None of the five things in our way needs a purchase, and none needs a laboratory.
