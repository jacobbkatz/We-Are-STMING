Abstract

We detected tunneling, but weren't able to maintain tunneling range for long enough to get an image.

Every subsystem a tunneling microscope needs is built, working and calibrated. The amplifier resolves picoamps, the chain agrees with theory to 0.13 of a standard error, and a real junction responds to voltage and to height.

Self-directed. No faculty advisor, no grant, no laboratory. Eleven weeks of planning and design, eight weeks of building. First power-on 29 August 2026; last bench session 19 September 2026. 29 session logs, 163 raw data files, 92 photographs of our own hardware and every analysis script are public — including the results we later withdrew.

Introduction

Two undergraduates. A basement. Our first soldering.

Neither of us writes code, and neither had much electronics experience — this project was the first real soldering either of us has done. It was built and run in the basement of a house people live in, with people moving around and no control over the air or the temperature. A wooden bench on a concrete floor, a few meters from the breaker panel. Not a lab. Not a clean room.

This is not a disclaimer. It is the result. A tunneling microscope is normally a clean-room instrument — and the blocker we found and measured is exactly the class of problem those rooms exist to remove. Stated up front, our limitation becomes a measurement of what this environment costs you.

Conditions stated by Jacob Katz, 2026-09-19.

Methods: The Instrument

We built the whole thing

There was no kit. We ordered the boards, printed the frame, adapted the firmware, and brought up every stage on its own before trusting the next one.

A Teensy 4.1 drives four 16-bit converters over a 1 MHz bus and a 26-way ribbon — X, Y and sample bias at ±3 V, Z at ±10 V — moving a piezoelectric disc that carries a cut tungsten tip. The tip current goes into a transimpedance amplifier with a 100 MΩ feedback resistor, in a hand-folded copper box, and is read back by a 16-bit LTC2326-16. Coarse approach is a 28BYJ-48 stepper on a lever. Mechanics, controller and firmware follow Mech Panda's red-panda-stm [1]; the scan head and preamplifier follow Dan Berard's home-built STM [2]. The electronics bring-up, the software, the calibration and the controls are ours.

Results: Calibration of the Measurement Chain

The measurement chain agrees with theory

We took the tip and sample out and clipped a known 100 MΩ resistor where the junction would be. That makes the current something Ohm's law fixes before the run — so the instrument is checked against a number it had no part in choosing.

This is the number everything else rests on It fixes the conversion — 320.5 counts per nanoamp — and it settles the sign, which matters because a current that drove the reading the unexpected way would send an automatic approach straight into the sample.

Results: A Real Junction

A real junction, with a barrier's signature

Getting a current is easy — push the tip in and there is plenty. The hard part is showing you have a barrier: two conductors with something in between that electrons have to cross.

And the electronics are not the limit With a junction live the noise floor is 9–19 counts; leakage through the tip lead is under 0.06 nA across ±2 V, more than 8 GΩ; and someone stamping on the floor two meters away moves one reading's spread from 131 to 132 pA. The building is not getting in electrically.

Limitations

What we do not claim

No image. Fifty-one recordings, none of them a demonstrable image of the sample — and our own controls are how we know. No controlled vacuum gap, which is the regime you need in order to scan: we measured tunneling through a barrier junction, and holding a gap open is a different thing that we have not shown. The 2026-09-19 junction was a pressed contact, measured. No atomic resolution and no scale bar anywhere — every axis is in converter counts, because this instrument has never established a distance scale of its own. No number is read off a photograph: captions are marked SAID or READ.

Results: Stability of the Gap

We found the limit, and measured it

To take an image the gap has to hold still while the tip sweeps. We stopped the motor, took our hands off the instrument, and watched where the surface went.

Why that ends the imaging question, in one comparison Over the five seconds one image takes, the gap drifts 396 to 1,722 counts. The "structure" in every candidate image we have is 29 to 458 counts. The thing being measured is smaller than the way the gap moves while it is being measured. Knowing that, precisely, is the result.

Results: Controls and Self-Audit

We built controls designed to kill our own results

Every scan was run again with the sideways sweep switched off. An X-held control has identical timing and identical loop behavior, but the tip never travels across the surface — so it cannot contain surface structure at all, by construction.

We audited ourselves, and published what we found At this pause point we re-derived our own published numbers from the raw files. Two statistics we had used to dismiss our own scans turned out to be computed across aborted files, and a reproducible feature we found most convincing turned out to be the feedback loop recovering from a jump, not the sample. Every conclusion survived. Two of the arguments did not — and both withdrawals are in the public record.

Discussion

We detected tunneling — and we know what stopped us holding it

Our best junction, two nights before the pressed contact, changed ten-fold about seven times faster: roughly 250 Z counts per decade.

We detected tunneling. We could not hold the range long enough to scan A tip cannot go from not touching to touching without passing through the separations where the only thing carrying electrons is tunneling — and we had bias applied and the amplifier recording the whole way, 60,928 readings inside that range across 109 approaches. The current is superlinear and symmetric and the junction never metallically shorted: a barrier, conducting by tunneling. What we have not done is hold that gap open and steady enough to scan. That turns on d, the tip's distance from the pivot line, which sets the lever ratio: it wants to be near 26 µm, and we have it bounded at under 0.5 mm. A microscope closes it in minutes.

The Second Instrument: The System That Runs the Science

Neither of us writes code. So the other thing we built was the framework that does — and, more to the point, the framework that catches it when it is wrong.

Two people, two computers, days apart. The failure that kills a project like this is not a hard experiment. It is a number corrected in one document and left standing in the eleven others that quote it.

That is not hypothetical here. On 2026-09-07 two constants changed, both were already written into more than a dozen documents, and correcting every copy anyone could think of still left stale values in six files.

So every number in this project now lives in exactly one place, carries a tag saying how it is known — MEASURED, CALC, DS, MESH, ORDER, SAID, READ — and is checked by a program, not by memory.

What proves it works is the retractions
  • A statistical result computed across truncated files — withdrawn, and the same defect then swept across every other comparison in the project.
  • Our most convincing reproducible feature — shown to be the feedback loop recovering from a flyback, predicted to the exact count. Withdrawn.
  • A detector's full-scale voltage recorded backwards in a live reference document. Caught by the checker.
  • Eleven wrong numbers in one commit, found by a verification pass and fixed before the second push.

An AI that writes confident prose is not hard to get. One that produces a retraction is, and that is the part we built. We did not train a model — the model is off the shelf. The discipline around it is ours.

Future Work

Where it goes next

  • Measure d properly — a microscope or comparator, no power, and it settles our best junction retroactively.
  • Prove the gap holds still before scanning anything — a box over the instrument, a 15-minute recording, a gate of 200 counts over sixty seconds.
  • A stiffer sample than gold leaf on paper, and a sharper tip.
  • Three free software fixes that remove the artifact behind our most convincing false positive: drop the first three pixels of each pass, sweep X both ways between passes, never compare two images of different length.
