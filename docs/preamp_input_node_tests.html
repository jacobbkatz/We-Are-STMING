The input node is on. Now prove it before you trust it

Eight continuity checks with nothing powered, then the current-limited ramp, then the hour of timed readings. The last one is the measurement this project has been trying to take for two weeks.

What changed, now that the resistor is fitted

Every previous plan said the smoke test came first and the node second. The node went on first, so the two have merged. That is not a problem, and it has one real advantage: with the 100 MΩ in place the amplifier has feedback, so the output should no longer sit at a rail. The very first power-up now tells you both things at once, whether anything is shorted and roughly how much leakage there is.

It also means a fault found now is a fault reworked on a finished node. So do all eight unpowered checks before the supply goes anywhere near it. They cost five minutes and they catch the three mistakes that would otherwise look like a catastrophic leak.

Top, chip side. Five holes on your right, the standoff at the left edge. Black probe stays on the standoff post for checks 1 to 6 — that is the black ring. Move only the red probe. Check 7 is the odd one out: the black probe moves to PAD1 for it.

The board is to scale. The node is not. The resistor and the link are drawn schematically, because how you routed them is your choice and I have not seen it. What the drawing is accurate about is where the three points are and what the resistor has to pass over to get there.

The standoff rests on the board surface. It is not in the hole. The 11301 would not enter our Ø2.108 mm hole, so the whole node is held by the 100 MΩ’s own lead in tension. That is sound electrically and fragile mechanically, which is what check 8 is about.

1 — Eight checks, nothing powered

The standoff is not in the hole. It would not enter, so it rests on the board surface held by the resistor lead. Electrically that is fine, and arguably better — no bare input-node metal reaches the underside. Mechanically it is fragile, which is why check 8 exists and why nothing should touch the board once the hour starts.

Meter on continuity. Black probe on the standoff post for 1 to 6. Handle the board by its edges: it has just been cleaned and a fingerprint at this node undoes the wash.

1  Standoff post ↔ IC1 pin 2 MUST BEEPProves the link is on and that the enamel came off both ends. An open here is the most likely fault tonight, and once powered it looks exactly like a catastrophic leak: the output rails, because an open feedback path is an infinite feedback resistor.
2  Standoff post ↔ IC1 pin 3 MUST BE SILENTPin 3 is ground and it is the very next leg down. A beep here shorts the amplifier’s input to its own reference. Hard stop — magnifier on those two legs.
3  Standoff post ↔ hole 1, the white GND lead MUST BE SILENTThe same fault seen from further away. Catches the link sagging onto anything grounded, not just onto pin 3.
4  Standoff post ↔ hole 2, the grey +15 V lead MUST BE SILENTCatches the resistor or its lead touching IC1 pin 7, the positive supply.
5  Standoff post ↔ hole 5, the tan −15 V lead MUST BE SILENTThe resistor passes closest to pin 4, the negative supply, on its way over the chip. This is the row that catches it.
6  Standoff post ↔ PAD1 OPEN OR VERY HIGHNot a beep. A beep means solder has bridged across the 100 MΩ. Your meter cannot read 100 MΩ — it tops out far below that — so open is the pass, and it proves only that the resistor is not shorted. Its real value is proved by the hour, not by this.
7  PAD1 ↔ hole 3, the orange OUT lead ABOUT 220 ΩThe black probe moves to PAD1 for this one. That 220 Ω is R1. It passed in Part B; repeat it to prove nothing was knocked loose while you worked.
8  Look at the standoff from the side, with a magnifier NOTHING TOUCHING, NOTHING LEANINGNot a meter reading. The standoff is not in the hole — it rests on the surface, held only by the resistor lead, so the whole node is a spring. The metal pole stands about 6 mm tall and IC1’s nearest leg is about 2 mm away, so a lean toward the chip can reach ground. Check the pole is upright and clear, the resistor body touches nothing, and neither lead rests on a pin. Then stop handling the board. Do not move it or its leads once the readings start.

If 1 fails, or 2 to 5 beep, stop

Do not power it. A bridge to ground or to a supply rail is minutes to find now with a magnifier, and an hour of confusing readings later. Send me which row failed.

18 V is the controller's number, not this board's

If your supply is set to 18 V, turn it down before you connect anything. That figure is real and it is in our own notes, but it belongs to the controller PCB, whose regulators need headroom above 15 V. This preamp board is not that board.

The controller feeds the preamp 15 V, and 15 V is what we are copying here. Our own parts notes give the op-amp's supply range as up to 18 V, so 18 V is the very top of what it will take. Sitting exactly on a part's limit leaves no margin, and supplies can overshoot for an instant when you switch them on. There is nothing to gain: the board will never see more than 15 V in normal use.

2 — Powering it, step by step

Two instruments, two different readings. Keep them straight. The power supply's own display shows current in milliamps. That is the safety check. The multimeter shows DC volts on the orange lead. That is the measurement we actually came for.

Never put the meter in current mode on this board

Not from white to orange, not anywhere. A multimeter in current mode is almost a piece of wire. Across white and orange it shorts the amplifier's output to ground through the 220 ohm resistor. If the output happened to be sitting at a rail, that is about 59 mA, more than the chip can supply, so it would go into its own limit and get hot.

It would also tell you nothing. The current we care about is around a nanoamp, and no multimeter can read that. That is the entire reason this amplifier exists. The 100 MΩ resistor turns that tiny current into a voltage large enough to read: one nanoamp in becomes 0.1 V out. So we read volts, and divide.

Check the red lead is in the volts socket, not the amps socket. A lead left in the amps socket and then touched across the supply is a dead short through the meter.

  1. Set both channels to 5.00 volts, limit 20 mA

    Not 18 V, and not 15 V yet. We start low so that if something on the board is shorted, far less energy goes into it before you notice.

    The 20 mA limit is already right. Leave it.

    Why 5 V and not straight to 15: a short at 15 V with a 20 mA limit still puts 0.3 W into whatever is shorted. At 5 V it is 0.1 W. Neither is dramatic, but the cheaper test goes first.

  2. Prove the three supply terminals are what you think

    Leads still off the board. Outputs on. Meter on DC volts.

    Black probe stays on GND. Then touch the red probe to each of the other two:

    Red probe onMeter should read
    +5 V terminal+5.0
    −5 V terminal−5.0, with the minus sign showing

    If the minus sign does not appear, stop. It means you have two positive supplies, not a positive and a negative one, and connecting that to the board would put 5 V backwards across it.

    Outputs off again. Now wire the board, in the next step.

  3. Wire the board, with the outputs off

    You have four leads on the board and only three places to put them. The orange one does not go to the supply at all.

    SupplyBoard leadWhat it is
    +5 Vgreypositive rail, hole 2, chip pin 7
    GNDwhite0 V, hole 1, chip pin 3
    −5 Vtannegative rail, hole 5, chip pin 4
    nothingorangethe amplifier’s output. It goes to the multimeter, never to the supply

    Connect white first, then grey, then tan. Coming apart later, reverse it: tan, grey, white last.

    If your supply does not have a GND terminal of its own, make one: join channel A’s black terminal to channel B’s red terminal with a short wire. That junction is your GND. Channel A’s red is then +5 V and channel B’s black is −5 V.

    Swapping grey and tan is the one mistake that breaks things. It puts both supplies backwards across the chip and across both tantalum capacitors. Check it twice before you switch on.

  4. Set the multimeter up on the orange lead

    Meter on DC volts. Not current. Not continuity.

    Black probe on the ground junction you made in step 3. Red probe on the free end of the orange lead.

    Clip them if you can, so you are not holding the board while it reads.

    The orange lead is the amplifier's output. It reads the same node as PAD1, through the 220 ohm resistor, and that resistor makes no difference to a voltage reading.

  5. Outputs on. Look at the supply, not the meter

    What you are reading: the current display on each channel, in mA.

    Expect a few mA on each, and the two roughly equal. The current runs from the positive rail, through the chip, and out the negative rail, so both channels carry the same current.

    Pass: neither channel sitting at 20 mA, neither number climbing, nothing warm to the touch.

    Ignore the volts at this step. At 5 V the output cannot swing far enough for the number to tell you anything. It is not a fault, it is just not a measurement yet.

    I still cannot give you an exact expected current. The op-amp datasheet is blocked from this session and we have never confirmed which of the two candidate chips is fitted. The pass above does not need that number.

  6. Switch off at once if

    A channel sits at 20 mA. The current keeps rising. Anything is warm.

    If both channels pin at 20 mA together, switch off and tell me. Do not raise the limit.

  7. Now 15.0 V on both channels. Never higher

    Turn both channels up to 15.0 V. Leave the limit at 20 mA.

    Check the two current displays again, same pass test as before.

    From here the multimeter reading is the real measurement. Note the time. This is minute zero.

What the number means

The output voltage divided by 100 MΩ is the current going into the amplifier. Read the size and ignore the sign.

under 0.1 Vunder 1 nAThe rebuild worked. This is the first valid input-current figure this project has ever had, and it clears half of safety rule 14.
0.1 to 1 V1 to 10 nAUsable but not clean. A tunnelling current would sit on top of this rather than under it. Worth chasing, not a disaster.

Standing rules for this board

Bench supply only. It does not meet the controller until C2 is replaced with a fresh part and the output reads under 0.1 V at 45 minutes. Both, not either. Safety rule 14.

Never above 15.0 V. Nobody within a metre during the hour. No cyanoacrylate near this board, ever.
