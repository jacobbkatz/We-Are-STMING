Verified 2026-09-14 — all nine readings pass

IC1 pin 3 beeps to hole 1. The first time in this project the amplifier’s 0 V reference has been connected to anything. All four ground wires make, the input is not shorted, neither rail is shorted, and hole 1 to hole 5 reads 4.7 µF — so C2 survived the two-iron rework.

The +15 V hazard at dot 4 is closed. Hole 2 read silent with wire 4 fitted, where a bridged tail would have held a beep.

Next, and still unpowered: continuity at the four lead free ends, then the current-limited smoke test — both channels 5.0 V, 20 mA limit, metered before anything is connected. Not the controller. The readings below are kept as the record, and as the list to repeat if anything on this board is ever reworked.

The eight points, in reading order

Black probe in hole 1, and leave it there. Everything below is where the red probe goes. The badge colour on the drawing is what the meter should do, so you can check a reading without looking away from the board.

On 6 and 7, expect nothing to happen. A continuity tester drives a roughly constant current, so while a capacitor charges the resistance it computes is t / C and it beeps for C × R_threshold — the test current cancels out. At 4.8 µF that is 96 µs to 960 µs, and a beeper needs about a millisecond to sound. Silence is the normal reading. Only a beep that holds means anything.

  2. One capacitance reading, and it is the strongest of the lot

    Meter on capacitance. Hole 1 to hole 5. Expect about 4.7 µF.

    That is C2 at 4.7 µF in parallel with C4 at 0.1 µF. Before today it read near zero — both of those capacitors had their ground ends hanging in the air.

    Readings 2 and 3 have already proved the wiring, so what this one tests is C2 itself, the part that came off and back on with two irons. Three outcomes: about 4.7 µF and C2 survived; about 0.1 µF and C2 has gone open, leaving only C4; near zero and something has come adrift since reading 3.

Why hole 2 is the one that settles it. The 0.51 mm gap is the scraped ring to the pad — the one you could see, and it is genuinely roomy. The 0.076 mm gap is the tail end to the same pad, under the mask. Now that wire 4 ties that node to hole 1, a bridge at the tail is a dead short from +15 V to ground, and the beeper will hold instead of chirping. Nothing subtle about it.

Once it passes, stop there

  • Do not power it, not even briefly When the checks pass, the next powered step is a bench supply on both channels with a 20 mA limit, set and metered before anything is connected — not the controller. And when it is powered: PAD1 will sit near a supply rail. That is correct, not a fault. The 100 MΩ feedback resistor is not fitted, so the amplifier has no feedback path yet.
  • Do not press the standoff in Two separate reasons, either one enough. The board has to be washed first — IPA scrub, clean IPA rinse, distilled water rinse, then dried — because you cannot clean underneath a fitted standoff, and that spot is the input node. And we have never measured the part. Put calipers on the standoff’s shank and on the board hole and send me both numbers. Our own notes mark the 11301’s mounting-hole figure disputed: 0.086″ is listed both as its hole and as its turret head, and both cannot be right. The board hole is 2.108 mm. Press an oversize shank into it and the fracture goes through the most sensitive point on the instrument.
  • Do not connect this board to the controller C2 is the original part, reused after a two-iron removal that almost certainly went past the 3 second limit. It gets replaced with a fresh 4.7 µF part, and PAD1 has to read under 0.1 V at 45 minutes on the bench supply, before the two ever meet.

What is actually wrong with these boards. Berard’s Eagle design has a ground copper pour across the whole underside. The KiCad gerbers JLCPCB built ours from have no filled regions at all, so six ground points each end at a dead via. Nobody here caused it.
