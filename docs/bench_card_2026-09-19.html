1

Stick the gold down

Unpowered · plate off the instrument · this is a gate
Why this is first. An unstuck gold leaf is pulled toward the tip by the bias at 58 times its own weight at a micron gap, and 5,800 times at 100 nm — and the pull grows as the gap shrinks, so it runs away and snaps. That is exactly what last night measured. A surface that is not attached to anything cannot be imaged.
  1. Take the plate off. Lift the old loose sheet away — gloves on, and do it away from the scan head.
  2. Lay fresh copper tape, adhesive side under the gold. That is how the 17 September patch was done, and that one did not move.
  3. Lay the leaf on, then burnish it: press and smooth with something soft — cotton ball, soft brush — in small circles.
  4. Keep going until it changes from dull and loose-looking to bright and flat. That change is how you know it has taken.
Pass
You blow on it and nothing moves.
Fail
Any movement at all. Burnish more, or re-lay it.
Do not go past this step until the blow test passes. Everything after it — the approach, the calibration, the noise, the image — is measuring a surface that is moving on its own.
Loose gold flakes are conductive dust. A flake landing on the tip holder or inside the preamp is a short across the amplifier input. Do the peeling and burnishing away from the head, and look for strays before the plate goes back on.

Write down two things afterwards: what is underneath the sheet, and that you burnished it. Neither is recorded for the current one.
2

Hold a magnet near the metal stake

Ten seconds · unpowered
Why. There are four disc magnets in the sample plate, a millimetre or two behind the gold — from Mech Panda's design, meant to hold a magnetic sample puck you do not use. The tungsten tip is already cleared: tungsten is barely magnetic, and the magnets' field varies over millimetres while the approach happens over microns, so there is no runaway. But the stake the holder is soldered to has never been identified.
  1. Bring a spare magnet near the metal stake behind the piezo. Gently — do not let it snap onto anything.
No pull
Not magnetic. One hypothesis closed for free.
Pulls
It is steel, and the magnets tug on the holder. Tell Claude — it becomes a second snap mechanism.
Either way, write the stake's material into the inventory. Nobody has ever recorded it.
3

Watch the plate while the motor runs

A few minutes · power on the motor only · eyes, not numbers
Why. Last night one motor step sometimes moved the surface 14,000–29,000 Z counts, other times 20 to 40 steps moved nothing, and twice a retract step brought the gold into contact. The leading mechanical explanation is that the plate sticks and slips on its three ball supports. The 7-turn hand-set changed how it sits.
  1. Plate back on, tip well clear. Send the motor a few hundred steps each way.
  2. Watch three things: does the motor screw's ball stay touching the plate? Does the plate rock on the two side-by-side screws? Does it move smoothly or in jerks?
If it jerks or the ball lifts, that is the fault, and it has to be fixed before any calibration means anything. More rubber-band tension is the cheap first thing to try — that is what fixed the same class of problem on 17 September.
4

Make and fit a sharper tungsten tip

However long it takes · unpowered
Tungsten is the right material — it is the standard STM tip metal, it is stiff (about twice steel), and it is not meaningfully magnetic. Keep using it. The current one is blunt, bent, and has taken about a dozen hard contacts.
  1. Make it short. A shorter tip is a stiffer tip, and stiffness is what stops a snap.
  2. Fit it. If you open the holder, put the tip as close to the centre of the piezo disc as you can — a disc flexes most at its middle, so a central tip gets the most Z travel and the least tilt.
No superglue anywhere near the piezo or the preamp. The tip holder is part of the amplifier's input node, and glue there is the fault that cost this project weeks.
5

Power up, and check the lights

Two minutes
  1. Supplies on first, then the USB.
  2. Look at LED1–LED4. They light at every power-on. RSET clears them.
  3. Send RSET, then park Z at the retracted end and bias at midscale.
  4. Let it sit about two minutes.
Good
All four LEDs dark after RSET. Tip clear, readings within a few hundred counts of zero.
Stop
Any LED still lit. Every reading taken with one lit is void, and no software can detect it.
Last night's noise with the tip clear was 7× worse than 17 September — about 341 counts against 46 — and it never recovered. If it is still high after the gold is fixed, that is worth chasing on its own: plate off, then bias wire off, reading each time.

Approach

Hands away from the head 
py Code/pc/stm_approach.py --z-retracted low \
   --motor-toward-sample negative --motor-step 5 \
   --max-steps 600 --z-step 200 -y
Negative approaches. Positive retracts. Settled 17 September, and it was backwards for two sessions before that.
  1. Sit back. A hand 5 cm from the head adds 370 pA — about a third of a tunnelling current. Sitting at the bench normally is fine; reaching in is not.
  2. When it reports contact, flip the bias before believing it. Negative sample voltage should read positive, positive should read negative, and both about the same size.
Real
The reading follows the sign of the bias. Noise cannot do that.
False
Same sign both ways, or it only appeared when someone moved. It has fooled us twice.

9

Load the suspension, then the stamp test

Ten minutes · the biggest single win available right now
Corrected 2026-09-19. Your springs are not too stiff — they are SHUT. A close-wound extension spring does not stretch at all until the load beats its initial tension, and yours needs roughly 600 to 900 g. At the rate these springs actually have, the 2 mm of droop you measured would mean the whole hanging assembly weighs 35 g — there are 50 g of nickels in the cups alone, so that is impossible. They have not opened. Your own words on the 18th were right: "these springs are more strechy than I thought adding more wieght could help I think." Once they open, they give about 2 Hz — real isolation, roughly a five-fold cut in your worst noise.
CHECK ZERO, AND DO IT BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE: IS THE PLATFORM SITTING ON THE TOWER? In your side-on photos the platform looks like it is resting on the damping stack — no daylight between them. That is a reading of a photograph, not a measurement, but if it is right the springs carry almost nothing and it explains the whole thing on its own. Try to slide a slip of paper into the gap. If it will not go, the platform is sitting on it — free it, and that is the biggest single win in the project, for nothing. Eddy damping needs an air gap; a plate resting on its magnets is a clamp, not a damper.
THEN CHECK THESE THREE. The platform will drop about 25 mm once the springs open.
  1. Clearance. Is there 30 to 50 mm of free space under everything that hangs?
  2. Wire slack. The orange coax, the loom, the stepper leads. A wire pulled taut across the suspension is a rigid bypass — it kills the isolation completely and can pull on the preamp input.
  3. The eddy-damping plate. If the platform drops 25 mm, does the aluminium plate leave the magnet gap or hit the magnets?
  1. Measure the droop now, before adding anything. Lift until the springs go slack, release, measure the drop.
  2. Add mass in stages — roughly 100 g at a time — and write down the droop after each one. Quarters do not fit the cups (they are wider than the cup's outside); fasten the mass to the platform some other way, and fasten it, do not rest it.
  3. Stop at about 1.2 kg total, or sooner if anything runs out of clearance.
  4. Then the stamp test: one measurement with the room still, one while someone stamps on the floor a couple of metres away.
Droop stays flat, then takes off
That knee is the initial tension, and finding it is the whole point. Past it you get about 5.7 mm more droop per 100 g.
Droop rises from the very first gram
Initial tension is small and something else was carrying the weight — a taut cable, the platform touching the tower, the damping plate resting on a magnet. Find it and free it. No amount of mass helps until it is free.
Noise jumps on the stamp
The building is getting through, and loading the springs is exactly the fix. Do not buy springs — the ones fitted are fine.
No change on the stamp
The shaking is made inside the instrument. The tip holder and the sample plate are the suspects.
Adding weight is blocked anyway until the damping magnets can come down with the platform.
10

Park and power down

In this order
  1. Back the tip off a few hundred motor steps.
  2. Park Z, X, Y and bias at midscale.
  3. USB out first, then the supplies off.
  4. Write down the motor step count. The firmware forgets it at every restart — it only exists where you write it.

Do not

  • Never send APRH. It compares against a baseline that has been the wrong sign all project, so it can drive the tip into the sample without ever triggering.
  • Never send CCON with the tip in range until you and Nuh decide to lift that rule.
  • Never send a Z value outside 0–65535. It does not error — it wraps and slams the axis to the opposite rail.
  • Never read a meter's "OL" as ruled out. It only proves more than about 60 MΩ, and what matters here is 100 MΩ to 10 GΩ.
  • No cyanoacrylate near the preamp or the piezo.
  • Do not judge the piezo by ear. There is no usable resonance when it is mounted.

One rule that has a deliberate exception

  • The written rule says park Z at midscale before the motor moves. Last night's session parked it at the fully retracted end instead and wrote down why: the high end of Z extends the tip toward the sample, so midscale leaves the tip half-extended while the motor is running. The retracted end serves the rule's purpose better.

    Use the retracted end tomorrow. Whether to reword the rule itself is yours and Nuh's call, not Claude's.
