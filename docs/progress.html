Build state · 16 September 2026 · bring-up day 19

We Are STMing

Two people, neither of whom writes code, are building a microscope that sees at the scale of single atoms — out of a 3D printer, a hobby microcontroller, and a hand-folded copper box.

This picture is a drawing, not a measurement. It is what an atomic surface looks like when a scanning tunneling microscope traces it, line by line — the thing we are building towards. We have not imaged anything yet. Everything below is the work of getting to the point where we can.

01 — what the machine does

A microscope with no lens, no light, and one atom for an eye

You cannot see an atom with light. Atoms are far smaller than the wavelength of light itself, so no lens, at any price, will ever resolve one.

A scanning tunneling microscope gets around that by not looking at all. It feels. A needle is sharpened until it ends in a single atom, and brought so close to a surface that the two are almost touching — but not quite. The gap left between them is about a nanometre: roughly three atoms wide.

At that distance something strange happens. Electrons, which classically have no business crossing an empty gap, simply appear on the other side. This is quantum tunneling, and it is not a metaphor — it is a measurable electric current of about one nanoamp, a billionth of an amp.

The useful part is how violently that current reacts to distance. Move the needle a fraction of an atom's width closer and the current leaps. That ferocious sensitivity is the whole instrument: drag the needle across a surface, keep the current constant by moving the needle up and down, and the up-and-down movements trace out the shape of the surface — bump by bump, atom by atom.

That current has no business existing. It is worth understanding why, because the whole instrument is built on it.

02 — the physics, plainly

What “tunneling” actually means

Roll a ball at a hill. If it does not have the energy to get over the top, it rolls back down. Every time. Without exception. That is the world we live in.

An electron is not a ball. It does not have a position in the way a ball does. What it has is a wave — a spread-out description of where it might turn up, taller where it is more likely and flatter where it is less.

And here is the part that matters: when that wave meets a barrier it cannot cross, it does not stop dead at the edge. It fades into the barrier. It gets rapidly smaller — but it does not become nothing. If the barrier is thin enough, a little of the wave is still there when it reaches the far side, and carries on.

A wave on the far side means a real chance of finding the electron there. Not a large chance. But an electric current is made of enormous numbers of electrons, so a tiny chance each, multiplied out, becomes a small, steady, entirely measurable current — flowing across a gap that nothing should be able to cross.

That is quantum tunneling. It is not a trick or an approximation; it is how the world works at that scale, and it has been measured for a century. Our microscope is a machine for listening to it.

Why this makes such a good ruler

The fade across the gap is brutally steep. As a rule of thumb, pulling the needle back by just 0.1 nm — about a third of the width of a single atom — drops the current by roughly a factor of ten. REF

That is the property the entire instrument is built around. A current that changes tenfold over a third of an atom is an exquisitely sensitive distance gauge. Hold the current steady and you are holding the gap steady to a small fraction of an atom's width — which is exactly how a machine with no lens ends up tracing the bumps of individual atoms.

That tenfold figure is a standard textbook result, not something we measured — hence the REF mark. The real gold atoms we will be scanning are about 0.29 nm across.

04 — how it works

The loop that holds the gap

Nothing in this instrument holds still by being sturdy. It holds still by constantly correcting itself, thousands of times a second, around a loop with five parts. Every one of those parts now works.

06 — the proof

The night the whole chain worked

You cannot test a machine like this by pointing it at something and hoping. So we faked the atom.

A single resistor, clipped in exactly where the needle and the surface will be, produces a current of precisely known size — the same size a real tunneling gap would. Sweep the voltage across it, and simple arithmetic says what should come out the other end of the instrument: −3,200 counts for every volt applied.

We measured −3,205. Fifty-three readings. Every one of them on the line.

07 — why this is hard

Three numbers that explain the difficulty

It is worth knowing what is actually being fought here. None of these are exaggerations; all three are worked out in the project's own notes.

330×

One degree of room temperature

A single degree Celsius expands the steel in the frame by about 330 nm — 330 times the width of the whole gap. Only the mismatch between the two arms of the frame actually moves the gap, so it is not 330 nm of real drift, but it is the scale of what the design has to cancel out. CALC

1 nA

The signal, against a human body

A person standing within a metre of the amplifier injects tens of nanoamps into it just by existing. The signal being hunted is about one. Every measurement that matters is taken with nobody near the bench. MEAS

0.01 nm

The step the scanner must take

To trace a surface atom by atom, the needle has to move in steps far finer than an atom is wide — and hold each one steady. Our scanner's smallest step is 0.0104 nm, which is just inside what is needed. INFER

The whole instrument hangs on springs, damped by an aluminium plate moving through magnets without touching them, because ordinary building vibration is enormous compared to a nanometre.

09 — how we got here

Eleven weeks of building, nineteen days of switching on

The instrument was designed, printed, ordered and wired before a single volt went through it. At the first power-up the build notes record the hardware as “fully built and wired” — everything after that date is bring-up.

Every working session since is written up the same night — the measurements, the dead ends, and what was ruled out. The two best days were the two where we found out we had been wrong.

3 Jul

The parts go to the printer

The five print plates are sliced — frame, scan head, isolation stack, enclosures. They specify carbon-filled nylon, which proves too difficult to print reliably at these tolerances; carbon-filled PETG is what actually gets built.

5 Jul

The amplifier board goes to manufacture

Manufacturing files generated for the preamplifier — a board 20 mm long carrying six components. The missing ground layer that would cost two weeks in September is already in these files, and nobody would find it for another ten weeks.

2 Aug

Parts ordering

Components on order. Among them a PTFE standoff for the amplifier's input node — which would turn out, six weeks later, to be the wrong part number and not fit the board at all.

Mid-Aug

Printed, populated, wired

Print material settled at carbon-filled PETG. Five controller boards come back assembled from the fab at about $64 each, and the amplifier boards come back populated. The scan head, the isolation platform and every connection go together by hand, by two people who had never soldered before this project.

29 Aug

The first volt

Nothing had ever been powered before this day. Months of printing, ordering, soldering and wiring, and then the real question — does any of it work? — gets asked for the first time. The controller board comes up and is characterised.

31 Aug

One thing works, two things don't

Voltage reaches the sample for the first time. In the same session the amplifier is found to be leaking badly, and both data buses are found running forty times faster than the ribbon cable can carry.

5 Sep

Two ways to destroy a needle, found by reading the code

Engaging the feedback loop would have slammed the scanner to the middle of its range from wherever it happened to be. Both faults were inherited from the original design. Both were found before any needle was fitted.

7 Sep

A multimeter overturns the project's headline number

The amplifier's leakage is three times worse than recorded — and the reference the whole measurement was taken against turns out to be floating. Every reading so far had been measured against a moving zero.

8–9 Sep

We stop, and audit ourselves

Two corrected numbers are found still sitting wrong in six live documents. We build one canonical file of every number that matters, and an automatic check that refuses to accept new work while a known-wrong value is still written anywhere. Its first run finds a real bug in our own analysis code.

13 Sep

The root cause, after two weeks of chasing symptoms

The amplifier's reference pin is connected to nothing — on both boards. The manufactured circuit boards were missing a ground layer that the original design has. Six connection points were dead from the day they arrived. Every amplifier measurement in the project had been meaningless, and we had spent two weeks trying to explain the readings.

14 Sep

Repaired by hand, with wire

Four wires onto a circuit board 20 mm long, then nine separate checks, all passing. The first bench session in the project that ended with no new faults.

15 Sep

The amplifier works

In its copper box, the repaired board settles at about 4 picoamps of error — 250 times better than the pass mark we had set, against a signal of 1 nA it has to find. A 45-minute warm-up rule we had obeyed for a week is thrown out: that drift had been the broken reference charging up, not the amplifier.

16 Sep

End to end — and the first approach

The known current goes in and the right number comes out. Both needle-destroying software faults are fixed and tested. A needle goes in for the first time, and the machine is told to go looking for the surface.

The record starts on 3 July, because that is the earliest dated file in the repository. The design, the research and the CAD work came before that and is not dated anywhere — so rather than guess, this page begins where the evidence does.

10 — the honest ledger

What we still cannot do

This project runs on a rule: a confident wrong number costs more than an admitted gap. So here is the gap.

We detected tunneling — but could not hold the range long enough to imageOpen

Corrected 2026-09-20. This entry used to read “we have never seen a tunneling current”, describing a first approach that moved the screw two thousand steps one way and six thousand the other and found nothing. That was true of that approach and wrong as a statement about the project.

The tip cannot travel from not-touching to touching without passing through the gap widths where tunneling is the only thing carrying electrons — and the bias was on with the amplifier recording the whole way. 60,928 readings were taken inside that current range, across 109 separate approaches. The current’s shape says the same: it climbs far faster than the voltage does, it is symmetric in both bias directions, and the junction never shorted metal-to-metal. That is a barrier, and a barrier conducting at 16–59 MΩ is conducting by tunneling.

What stays open is holding it. To scan a surface you need the gap to sit still, and ours moves by most of the scanner’s whole range within seconds with nothing touching the instrument. That is the unsolved problem, and it is mechanical rather than electrical. Nothing was damaged.

Which way the motor approachesDisputed

The CAD model says one direction. Jacob, looking at the actual machine, reads it the other way. The needle sits 1 mm from the pivot — too fine a margin to settle on paper, so it gets settled by looking, with a ruler in the photograph.

Which way the scanner extendsUnknown

It depends on how the piezo disc was polarised at the factory, which nobody recorded. Rather than guess with a needle at risk, the software now searches both directions and reports which one it was at the moment of first contact.

The converters forget their settingsOpen

All four drop their configuration roughly once an hour. There is no way to detect it in software, so every measurement is bracketed by physically looking at four indicator lights on the board.

One evening of noise we cannot explainOpen

Noise got twenty times worse for part of one session, then went away. Two suspects were tested one variable at a time, and both were cleared. The leading candidate is untested.

11 — the machine

What it is made of

Frame
3D-printed in carbon-filled PETG. Not PLA — PLA slowly creeps under load, and creep at this scale shows up directly as a smear across the image
Vibration isolation
A 200 mm platform hung on three springs, damped by an aluminium plate moving through fixed magnets — no contact, no rubber, nothing to wear out MESH
Coarse approach
A stepper motor turning an 80-threads-per-inch screw through a lever, bringing the sample to within striking distance a few nanometres at a time
Scanner
A piezoelectric disc — a ceramic that changes shape when voltage is applied — carrying a tungsten needle cut at 45°. About 680 nm of travel INFER
Amplifier
An OPA627 with a 100 MΩ feedback resistor, in a hand-folded copper box mounted at the scan head. This is the part that has to see a nanoamp
Controller
A Teensy 4.1 hobby microcontroller, four 16-bit outputs and one 16-bit input
Sample
Gold leaf on a magnetic plate. We expect atomic terraces — flat regions separated by steps one atom high — rather than individual atoms, which are far harder on metals

“You have the data, not us. 100% should never happen again.” Jacob, 7 September — after being told to check a component's safe limits after being told to make the connection. The working rules were rewritten the same day, and the order was reversed permanently.
