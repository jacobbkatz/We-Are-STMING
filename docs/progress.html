We Are STMing

Two people who don't write code are building a scanning tunneling microscope out of a 3D printer, a Teensy and about £400 of parts. It is meant to image individual atoms. This is where it actually is — measured, not estimated.

01 — where it standsThe instrument can now feel a current

As of 16 September the whole signal chain works end to end. A known current injected where the tunneling gap will be travels through the amplifier, the cable, the converter, the firmware and the laptop, and comes out the far end as the right number. Every bring-up stage passes.

What has not happened yet: the tip has never touched the sample. The first approach attempt, the same evening, found no contact. That is the next thing.

02 — the proofThe night the whole chain worked

A tunneling current is about one nanoamp — roughly a billionth of the current in a phone charger. Proving you can measure something that small, before you risk a tip on it, means faking it: we clipped a 100 MΩ resistor across the gap where the tip and sample will be, swept the sample voltage across its full ±3 V range, and read the result back through the real amplifier, the real cable and the real converter.

Ohm's law says that should give −3,200 counts per volt. We measured −3,205 ± 37. The prediction and the measurement differ by 0.16%.

04 — how we got hereNineteen days, sixteen logged sessions

Every session is written up the same night, with the measurements, the failed approaches, and what was ruled out. The two hardest days were the two where we found out we had been wrong.

29–30 Aug

First power-on

The controller board is assembled and characterised. Draws 60 mA and 36 mA from its two rails — a number that mattered more than we knew at the time.

31 Aug

The sample bias path works — and the amplifier doesn't

First verified signal path in the project. In the same session the preamplifier is found to be leaking badly, and both converter buses are found running at 40 MHz down a ribbon cable that cannot carry it. Dropped to 1 MHz.

5 Sep

Two ways to destroy a tip, found by reading the firmware

Engaging the feedback loop snapped the Z axis to mid-range from wherever it was; the motor was left energised after every move, heating the scan head. Both faults were inherited from the upstream design, and both would have cost a tip. Found before any tip was fitted.

6 Sep

The manufacturing files turn out to contain the answers

The gerber archives we had been treating as pictures are plain text, and carry the complete netlist of both boards. A connector pinout we had marked "unknown, do not run a wire" was resolved from a file that had been sitting in the repository for days.

7 Sep

A meter overturns the project's headline number

The amplifier's leakage is three times worse than recorded, and the converter's reference input is discovered floating — meaning every reading taken so far had been measured against a drifting zero.

8–9 Sep

We stop and audit ourselves

Two corrected numbers are found still sitting wrong in six live documents. We build a single canonical file of every number that matters, and a checker that refuses a commit while a known-wrong value is still written anywhere it would be believed. Its very first run finds a live bug in our own analysis code.

13 Sep

The root cause, after two weeks of chasing symptoms

The amplifier's 0 V reference pin is connected to nothing — on both boards. The fabricated circuit boards have no ground plane: the original design has one, the files sent to the manufacturer do not, leaving six ground points dead. Every preamplifier measurement in the project had been taken on an amplifier with no reference. None of them were ever currents at all.

14 Sep

Repaired by hand, with wire

Four wires onto a board 20 mm long, then eight continuity checks and a capacitance check, all passing. The first bench session in the project that ended with no new faults.

15 Sep

The preamplifier works

Mounted in its copper box, the repaired board reads about 4 picoamps — 250 times inside the limit we had set as the pass mark, against a signal of 1 nA it has to detect. A 45-minute warm-up rule we had been obeying for a week is retired: that drift was the floating reference charging up, not the amplifier.

16 Sep

End to end, and both tip-crash faults fixed

The converter reads the amplifier. The dummy junction test passes at 320.5 counts per nA. The two firmware fixes are uploaded and bench-tested — deliberately reproduced on the old firmware first, so we could watch the check go red before it went green. A tip goes in, and the first approach is attempted.

05 — honest ledgerWhat we still can't do

This project keeps a rule that a confident wrong number costs more than an admitted gap. So:

No tunneling current yetOpen

The first approach ran the coarse screw 2,000 steps one way and 6,144 the other and found nothing. Four candidate explanations, none tested. Nothing was damaged.

Which way the motor approachesDisputed

The CAD mesh says one direction; Jacob, looking at the actual build, reads it the other way. The tip sits 1 mm from the pivot line MESH and the tip holder is improvised, so 1 mm is too thin a margin to settle it on paper. It gets settled by looking, with a ruler in the photo.

Which way the scanner extendsUnknown

It depends on how the piezo disc was polarised at the factory and which face the tip is on, and neither is recorded anywhere. Rather than guess, the approach tool now searches both directions and reports which one it was at first contact.

The converters lose their configurationOpen

All four drop their settings roughly hourly. We know how to detect it — four indicator LEDs — but there is no software way to catch it, so every measurement is bracketed by looking at the board.

One noise spike we cannot explainOpen

Noise got 20× worse for part of one evening and then went away. Two suspects were tested one variable at a time and both were cleared. The leading candidate — a freshly soldered, solvent-cleaned joint still drying — is untested.

How far one motor step actually moves the tipUnresolved

The lever ratio is 20, 30 or 40 depending on which source you believe, putting one step somewhere between 3.9 and 7.8 nm CALC. The measurement that settles it is the same photograph that settles the motor direction.

06 — the machineWhat it's made of

Frame
3D-printed in carbon-filled PETG for stiffness and dimensional stability. Not PLA — it creeps, and creep shows up directly as drift in the image
Vibration isolation
A 200 mm platform hung on three springs, damped by an aluminium plate moving through fixed magnets — no contact, no rubber MESH
Coarse approach
A stepper motor turning a 1/4"–80 screw through a lever, giving single-digit nanometres per step
Scanner
A piezoelectric disc with a tungsten tip cut at 45°. Roughly 680 nm of travel in Z, inferred from a similar disc, not ours
Amplifier
An OPA627 current-to-voltage converter with a 100 MΩ feedback resistor, in a hand-made copper box at the scan head. This is the part that has to see a nanoamp
Controller
Teensy 4.1, four 16-bit converters for X, Y, Z and sample bias, and a 16-bit converter reading the current back
Sample
Gold leaf on a magnetic plate, biased through the mounting magnet
“You have the data, not us. 100% should never happen again.” Jacob, 7 September, after being told to check a datasheet after making the connection. The working rules were rewritten the same day.

07 — creditStanding on other people's work

Neither of us invented any of this. The mechanics, firmware and controller board come from Mech Panda's red-panda-stm; the scan head, tip preparation and amplifier design come from Dan Berard's home-built STM, which is the reference build for anyone attempting this at home. Thanks also to Dr Percy Zahl of Brookhaven National Laboratory for guidance on vibration isolation and scan head design.

Everything we have worked out that wasn't already documented — and everything we got wrong on the way — is written up in the open, because the gap between "here are the files" and "here is how to actually get it working" is where we have spent almost all of our time.
