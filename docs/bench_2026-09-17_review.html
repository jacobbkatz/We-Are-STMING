01

The five things that actually happened

Ranked by how much they change what you do next.

1

You made a real junction, and it is a barrier rather than a short

Settled · measured

Current rose from 0.85 nA at 0.05 V to 31.7 nA at 0.5 V — about four times faster than a plain resistor would. It is symmetric in both bias polarities to within 10%. A metallic short would be a straight line; an open circuit would give nothing. This is the shape tunnelling has.

2

The motor was driving the wrong way the whole time

Settled · by a crash

Negative MTMV approaches; positive retracts. The design file was right and the reading of the lever at the bench was inverted. Every approach before 19:22 tonight, and all 8,000 steps on 16 September, drove the sample away from the tip. Cost: roughly two hours.

3

The sample plate was never touching the motor screw

Settled · fixed at the bench

Both rubber bands pulled in line with the two ball ends, so nothing held the motor end down and the screw turned in free space with about a millimetre of air in front of it. One more rubber band fixed it. This is the best explanation for 16 September's null result — a mechanical assumption nobody had ever written down, let alone checked.

4

There is no image, and the electronics are not why

The limit is mechanical

Amplifier noise, leakage, the feedback loop and the read path were each measured and each ruled out with a number. What remains is a junction being shaken below 30 Hz and a height channel that wobbles by as much as any picture in it. A rigid tip holder, a sharper tip and damping at 5–20 Hz are what stand between this instrument and an image.

5

One question was left genuinely open, and it costs ten minutes

Open · first job next session

At the widest scans there is a repeatable 636-count profile, bigger than the wobble floor. The control that would say whether that is the sample or the scanner's own bow was run at the wrong width and analysed unfairly, so it proved nothing. Re-running it properly is the first thing to do.

02

Is it a real junction?

Two independent shapes say yes. The current bends the right way against voltage, and it climbs the right way against height.

One caveat worth carrying: the tip was probably on copper, not gold

Asked at the end of the session where the tip was sitting, Jacob's answer was that it was probably over the copper tape beside the gold patch. Nothing measured tonight can tell them apart — both are conductive and both connect to the bias wire.

It matters because copper grows an oxide skin in air within hours and gold does not. An oxide film is exactly the “barrier you press into” that Figure 3 shows, and it would explain why the junction never fell below about 5 MΩ even when the amplifier saturated: the tip never metallically shorted, it was always conducting through something. Getting the gold under the tip is cheap and is step 1 next session.

03

Is it a tunnelling gap, or is the tip pressing on something?

This is the one the evening could not settle, and the reason is a single unmeasured distance.

Everything in nanometres hangs on one measurement nobody has taken

The tip sits somewhere near the line between the two side-by-side ball ends. How near decides the lever ratio, and the lever ratio converts every count on this page into a distance. The design file says 1.00 mm. Jacob's own reading is that it is “in the middle of the line”, which would be far closer. The two answers differ by a factor of ten, and they lead to opposite conclusions about whether tonight was tunnelling.

Measured at the bench, 2026-09-18, and it came back as a range: d is between 0 and 1 mm — Jacob, “its between 1mm and 0mm its really hard to measure”. So the lever ratio is 40 or better, and the design value is the worst case rather than the answer. Every row of the table below is still live.

It is not a precision measurement. It is a threshold, and the threshold is 0.13 mm

The current falls one decade per 50 to 100 Z counts at the onset. Vacuum tunnelling falls one decade per 0.1 nm. Those two only meet if d is under about 0.13 mm — 0.065 mm on the shallower of the two slopes. So the question is not “what is d” but “is the tip within a tenth of a millimetre of the line, or clearly further?”

At that end of the range everything becomes consistent at once. With d near 0.13 mm the height wobble of 287–419 counts works out at 0.6 to 0.8 nm — just under the nanometre at which a vacuum gap stops conducting. That is a machine that is tunnelling and being shaken out of it, which fits everything else measured: the barrier-shaped I–V, the ten-second collapse into contact, and the fact that the junction kept coming back.

At the other end, d = 1 mm, the same wobble is 4.4 to 6.5 nm — far too large for any gap to survive, and the junction has to be something the tip is resting on. The two ends of a range that is hard to see tell opposite stories, which is why this one number is still the most valuable measurement left in the instrument.

04

Is there an image in the data?

The honest answer is: no, except possibly at the very widest scans, where the question is still open. Here is the ruler that decides it.

The control that was supposed to settle this, and why it did not

The test is simple: run the same line at three separated Y positions. If the profile is the sample, three different places must give three different shapes. If it is the scanner, all three give the same shape.

It was run, and it reported that different places agreed better than one place agreed with itself — which killed the result. That comparison was broken in two ways. It compared five-pass averages between places against single passes within a place, and averaging raises a correlation all by itself. And it ran at ±8,000 counts while the result it was testing ran at ±15,000 — a width at which nothing reproduces anywhere, so it could only ever have returned “no effect”.

Recomputed like for like from the committed file: within a place r = +0.133, between places r = +0.146, a difference of 0.013. There is no effect either way. The question is open, not answered. This was caught by an independent review of the committed data rather than at the bench, and the correction is already in STATUS.md.

07

What to do next, in order

Three of these five need no electronics at all, and the two cheapest come first on purpose.

Re-run the three-Y control, at ±15,000 counts this time

The same line at three separated Y positions, five passes each, analysed single-pass against single-pass at both places. If the three places give different shapes, the 636-count profile is the sample and this project has its first topography. If they give the same shape, it is the scanner's bow and the matter is closed.

Ten minutes · settles the biggest open question on this page

Lay a straightedge across the two ball ends and measure where the tip is

Plate off, power off. There is a drawing of exactly this in section 03 — lay a straightedge across the two ball ends, then measure from the straightedge to the tip, and note which side of it the tip falls on. This single distance converts every count on this page into nanometres and decides whether tonight's junction was tunnelling at all. Tonight's arithmetic implies about 0.1 mm against the design file's 1.00 mm.

Five minutes · no electronics · sets every scale in the project

Get the gold under the tip — done 2026-09-18. Now prove it carries the bias

A second sheet was laid so the whole plate face is gold, so the tip lands on gold wherever it lands and the copper-oxide confound is gone. What replaces this step is one meter check: gold to the orange bias wire must beep. A fresh sheet laid over aluminium tape may not be joined to anything — aluminium tape's adhesive usually does not conduct, and if the gold is not on the bias then no tunnelling current can exist at all.

The prediction from 17 September is now testable: a junction on clean gold should give a more symmetric response to Z than −1.13 against +0.36 decades, and a steadier hold than 287–419 counts of wobble. If neither improves, the surface was never the limit and it is all mechanics.

One meter check · then it is a real prediction, so it is a real test

Measure the suspension's droop, and do the stamp test

There is a drawing of this just above. Lift the platform until the springs go slack and measure how far it rises: f₀ = 15.76 / √(droop in mm) Hz. 10 mm gives 5 Hz, which sits on the very worst of the measured noise; 50 mm gives 2.2 Hz, which is clear of it. Then run one scan with the room still and one while someone stamps on the floor a couple of metres away. If the noise jumps, the building is getting through the stage.

Five minutes · aims the vibration fix instead of guessing at it

Decide about the tip holder, and about closing the loop

Both are yours and Nuh's calls, not Claude's, and both are now the limiting items rather than anything electrical. The holder is improvised and bends under a meter probe. The firmware's own constant-current loop is still barred by safety rule 8, even though its bug was fixed and bench-tested on 16 September — and Figure 8's ten-second close is exactly the case for using it.

A decision, not a measurement

Two controls that cost nothing and would have saved an hour each

Before believing any image, run a scan with X held still and the same line at three separated Y positions. Both failed tonight, and either one alone would have caught the result early instead of after it had been half-believed. The X-held control in Figure 4 is the clearest thing on this page.
