Gold leaf onto copper tape, copper tape onto the plate

Parts 0 and 1 are two quick tests that decide whether the rest is worth doing — is the leaf really gold, and does the copper tape's glue conduct. Everything here is done with the power off and the sample plate off the instrument. Nothing in this card can damage anything electrically. The two things you can lose are the gold leaf and the tip — and both are protected by doing the work on the bench rather than on the microscope.

Read this first — it may be why the last approach found nothing

The gold has to be electrically joined to the bias wire, or no tunneling current can ever flow — the tip would come down, touch, and read nothing. That is one of the four untested explanations for the failed approach on 16 September.

The weak link is the glue on the copper tape. Some copper tape has conductive adhesive and some does not. Ours has never been checked — it is an open question in docs/INVENTORY.md. If the glue does not conduct, the gold sits on an island: it looks perfect, meters fine to itself, and is electrically connected to nothing.

That is what Part 1 tests, and it takes five minutes. Do it before you touch the gold.

Two hard rules for this job

1. No superglue. None. Not near the plate, not in the room with the amplifier open. Cyanoacrylate vapour travels and settles on the amplifier's input, and that is the part that took two weeks to fix. Standing safety rule 5. If something needs sticking, it is copper tape or nothing.

2. Loose gold flakes are conductive dust. A flake that lands on the tip holder or the amplifier bridges things that must stay apart. Work away from the scan head, on a sheet of paper you can fold up and bin afterwards.

KitWhat to have on the table

  • Your multimeter, set to continuity (the setting with the sound-wave symbol, that beeps)
  • Fine tweezers — in the inventory, you have these
  • Nitrile gloves — in the inventory. Wear them. Skin oil ruins gold leaf and contaminates everything it touches
  • The magnifier — you will not be able to judge flatness with the naked eye
  • Something smooth to rub the gold down with: a clean cotton ball, a soft make-up brush, or the back of a plastic spoon. Not your finger
  • Sharp scissors or a fresh scalpel blade, and a cutting surface
  • Copper tape UNKNOWN: whether ours has conductive adhesive. Part 1 settles it.
  • The gold leaf You have four 1-inch squares (Jacob, 17 Sept). That is about 100 usable 5 mm pieces — spend them freely. Still UNKNOWN: whether it is real gold at all, and whether it is loose or backed. Part 0 settles the first.
  • A lighter, steel tweezers and a ceramic tile — for Part 0
  • Household bleach and white vinegar, if you want the chemical confirmation. Never the two together, and never either one near the microscope
  • The sharpest scissors you own, or a fresh scalpel and a steel rule. Blunt scissors are the single most common reason a piece of leaf is ruined.
  • A sheet of clean white paper to work on, and a phone to photograph the plate before you remove it

Conditions

Still air. Gold leaf this thin moves in a draught you cannot feel. Close the window, turn off any fan, and do not talk over it or breathe on it. Turn the soldering iron off and let it cool — you will not need it unless Part 1 fails, and a hot iron near gold leaf is a good way to lose the leaf to convection.

Part 0Prove it is actually gold 10 MIN · AWAY FROM THE INSTRUMENT

Why it matters, and it is not vanity. Gold is the standard sample for this because it does not oxidise — a clean gold surface stays conductive in air forever. Imitation leaf — sold as Dutch metal, composition leaf or schlagmetal (all names for the same thing) — is brass, copper and zinc. Brass grows a tarnish layer within days, and that layer is an electrical insulator. You would be trying to tunnel through a film that does not conduct, on a surface that keeps changing. It would not stop you getting contact, but it would make every image untrustworthy.

Chemical safety — three hard rules

1. Never mix bleach with any acid, or with ammonia. Bleach plus acid makes chlorine gas. Bleach plus ammonia makes chloramine. Both are genuinely dangerous in a small room.

2. Never make aqua regia — nitric plus hydrochloric acid. It is the one mixture that does dissolve gold, so it cannot tell you anything, and it releases chlorine and nitrosyl chloride while it does it. There is no reason to make it for this test, ever.

3. Do every chemical test in a different room from the microscope, and wash your hands and tools before you go back to it. Acid mist, chloride and dissolved metal salts are exactly the contamination that makes an amplifier input leak — and this project has already lost two weeks to a leaking input once.

Test 1 — the flame test. Do this one. No chemicals at all.

  1. Take a scrap of leaf about 2–3 mm — a torn corner is perfect. Not a whole sheet.
  2. Hold it in the steel tweezers, over a ceramic tile or a sink, away from the gold leaf book (the tissue pages are flammable) and away from the instrument.

    Open a window. The quantities are tiny but brass gives off zinc oxide smoke.

  3. Pass a lighter flame over it for one or two seconds. Do not hold it in the flame and walk away.
  4. Read the result immediately.
    Stays bright yellow. May shrink or ball up. Does not darken — that is real goldGold
    Goes black or brown within a second, possibly a green flash and a wisp of white smoke — that is brassNot gold

    Why it works: copper oxidises to black copper oxide at a few hundred degrees, long before anything melts. Gold does not oxidise at any temperature. The colour change is the answer, not whether it melts.

If the leaf blows out of the tweezers, lay the scrap on a ceramic tile instead and bring the flame down onto it.

Test 2 — bleach spot test. Safe, and a good second opinion.

  1. Put a scrap on a glass saucer or a white tile.
  2. One drop of ordinary household bleach on it. Gloves on.
  3. Watch for five minutes.
    Unchanged, still bright — gold Gold
    Darkens, dulls or goes patchy — brass Not gold
  4. Rinse it away with plenty of water and wash your hands.

Test 3 — vinegar and salt. The gentlest thing that works.

A good pinch of table salt stirred into white vinegar makes a weak acid. A drop on a scrap will dull and etch brass over ten to thirty minutes, sometimes turning faintly blue-green as copper dissolves. Gold is untouched. Slower and less dramatic than the flame, and completely safe.

Test 4 — nitric acid. The jeweller's standard. Only if you already handle nitric.

Read before you reach for the nitric

This is the textbook test and it is completely decisive: one drop of concentrated nitric acid on a scrap. Brass fizzes, dissolves and turns the drop green-blue, giving off brown fumes. Gold does nothing whatsoever — that is what "the acid test" means.

The hazard is the brown fumes. That is nitrogen dioxide. It is acutely toxic, and the serious effect — fluid on the lungs — can be delayed for hours after you feel fine. It needs to be done outdoors or under real extraction, with splash goggles, and with gloves rated for nitric acid — ordinary nitrile is attacked by concentrated nitric.

The flame test gives you the same answer in two seconds with none of this. Only use nitric if you are already set up for it and want the confirmation.

Whatever the answer, write it down. If it is brass, that is a real finding and it changes the plan — not a failure. Tell me the result and I will record it in docs/INVENTORY.md, where it should have been all along.

Part 1Does the copper tape's glue conduct? 5 MIN

Why this first. Everything downstream depends on it, and it costs one scrap of tape. If you skip it and the answer is "no", you will spend tomorrow chasing a tunneling current that physically cannot exist.
  1. Cut two small scraps of copper tape, about a fingernail each.

    Peel the backing off both.

  2. Stick one scrap down on the paper, shiny copper side up, glue side down.
  3. Stick the second scrap on top of the first, overlapping by about half — so the second one's glue is pressed onto the first one's copper.

    Press it down firmly with your thumbnail through a bit of paper. Give it a good rub.

  4. Meter across the join. One probe on the exposed copper of the bottom scrap, the other probe on the copper of the top scrap.
    Glue conducts — the easy case Beeps
    Glue does not conduct — you must add a metal-to-metal bond, see below Silent

If it was silent — the fix, and it is easy

Nothing is wrong with the tape; you just cannot rely on the glue to carry the connection. You make the connection metal to metal instead. Either:

  • Fold-over: cut your tape long enough to wrap around the edge of the plate, so the copper on the front touches the copper already on the back. Press the fold hard.
  • Or solder: a single small blob joining the new tape to the copper already on the plate. Copper takes solder easily. Do this with the plate off the instrument and the iron nowhere near the amplifier, then let it cool completely.

Either way you re-meter afterwards and it must beep before you go on.

Part 2Take the sample plate off the instrument

Why. There is a tip fitted — blunt and bent, but fitted, and it is the thing that turns a fumble into a rebuild. Gold work needs two hands, tweezers and a rubbing motion. Do none of that within reach of the tip. The plan already calls for the plate to come off anyway.
  1. Confirm the power is off and the USB is unplugged. It should already be.
  2. Photograph the plate in place, from the front and from one side.

    You need to put it back the same way round. Get the rubber bands and which screws it sits on into the picture.

  3. Ease the rubber bands off and lift the plate away.

    Lift it straight back and away from the tip, not sideways past it.

  4. Carry it to a different part of the bench and set it on your sheet of paper.

    Far enough that gold flakes and tape offcuts cannot reach the scan head or the amplifier.

Part 3Fresh copper tape onto the plate

  1. Look at what is already there under the magnifier.

    If the existing copper is clean and flat where the tip will land, you can lay the gold straight onto it and skip to Part 4. If it is creased, dirty or lifting, put a fresh strip down.

  2. Wipe the area with IPA on a cotton bud and let it dry completely.

    You are removing finger oil. Do not soak it.

  3. Cut a strip of copper tape a bit bigger than the area you want gold on.

    Cut it with scissors on the paper. Handle it by the edges.

  4. Lay it down from one edge, rolling it flat as you go, so no air gets trapped.

    Then rub the whole strip down hard with the back of the spoon. Flat matters — a ripple is a hill the tip has to climb.

  5. Meter it now, before the gold goes on. One probe on the new copper, the other on the wire that runs to the sample plate.
    New copper → sample-plate wire Must beep

    Silent means stop and fix it with the fold-over or the solder blob from Part 1. Do not carry on and hope.

Which wire is the sample-plate wire?

It is the lead that lands on the sample plate at the scan head — recorded in our own bench log as the orange one at that end.

Do not confuse it with the orange wire in the DSUB cable, which is −15 V. The bias wire inside the DSUB2 cable is black; the orange is the local jumper at the plate. If there is any doubt about which physical wire you have hold of, stop and ask before you meter. Nothing is at risk from metering, but a wrong identification here quietly invalidates every reading that follows.

Part 4The gold leaf onto the copper

What you are aiming for. A flat patch a few millimetres across that is firmly held and electrically joined. You do not need the whole plate covered and you do not need it perfect. The tip will look at an area smaller than a full stop. One good flat patch beats a big wrinkled sheet.

The sheet is far bigger than you need — and that is the easy problem, not the hard one

Work out how small you can go. The scanner's whole range is about 0.5 micrometres across — roughly a hundred-and-fortieth of the width of a human hair. A 5 mm square of gold is already ten thousand times wider than anything the tip will ever look at.

You have four one-inch squares left, and that is not four attempts. It is about a hundred. A one-inch square is 25 mm, so it cuts into five by five = 25 pieces of 5 mm. Four squares is roughly 100 usable pieces. Spend them freely — the expensive thing tomorrow is your patience, not the gold.

Two words I used that you may not know

“Rub it down” (gilders say burnish) just means press and smooth it with something soft, in small circles, until it lies flat and sticks. A cotton ball or a soft brush. Not hard pressure — you are smoothing, not scrubbing.

“The shiny backing paper” is the waxy sheet on the back of the copper tape that you peel off and normally throw away. Don't throw it away — it is a perfect work surface, because tape lets go of it cleanly.

Getting a small piece — two ways, and both beat cutting loose leaf

The mistake is trying to cut a free-floating sheet. Unsupported leaf has no strength at all; scissors crumple it and tweezers tear it. Never cut leaf that is not sandwiched between something.

Way 1 — sandwich a square and cut through it. Do this first.

  1. Take ONE square out. Leave the other three sealed where they are.

    You are about to get 25 pieces out of this one. There is no reason to expose the rest.

  2. Lay it between two sheets of clean paper — printer paper is fine, baking parchment is better. If the squares came interleaved with tissue, use that tissue and do not remove it.

    You now have a paper-gold-paper sandwich. Handle only the paper from here on.

  3. Cut the whole sandwich into strips about 5 mm wide, with your sharpest scissors.

    You are cutting paper, gold, paper together. Blunt scissors crush and drag; sharp ones shear cleanly. A fresh scalpel against a steel rule on glass is even better.

  4. Cut the strips into squares, the same way. Leave them in the sandwich until the moment each one is used.

    Slide one out by tilting the paper, or peel the top sheet off just that one piece.

Way 2 — gold the tape first, then cut the tape. Best for a precise final size.

The trick: gold that is stuck down to copper is supported, and supported gold cuts like paper. So do the delicate part on an oversized piece where mistakes do not matter, then cut the result down.
  1. Cut a piece of copper tape about 15 mm square and lay it back onto the shiny backing paper it came off, copper side up.

    That shiny paper is made to let go cleanly, so it is a perfect temporary work surface — the tape will lift straight off it again when you want it.

  2. Put the gold onto that copper by whichever method below suits your leaf, and rub it down. Do not worry about the edges — you are going to cut them off.
  3. Cut the gold-on-copper down to 3–5 mm with sharp scissors, choosing the flattest, cleanest region to keep.
  4. Peel it off the shiny paper and stick it onto the plate.

    You now have a small, flat, pre-made gold sample with its own adhesive — and you never had to handle bare leaf at final size.

Keeping static under control

Gold leaf jumps to anything charged. Use metal tweezers and paper, not plastic tools, do not work in a fleece, and if the room is very dry a bowl of hot water nearby for ten minutes helps more than you would expect. Cut on glass or a ceramic tile, not on paper that sheds fibres.

Handling gold leaf — read this before you unwrap any of it

It is about a ten-thousandth of a millimetre thick. It tears if you look at it wrong, it will wrap itself around tweezers, and it sticks permanently to skin. The rules are: gloves on, never touch it directly, keep it between paper for as long as you possibly can, and move slowly.

The transfer, in one method — the flip

The whole idea in one sentence: never let the gold touch anything except paper and the copper. You keep it on its paper, turn the paper upside-down onto the copper, rub the back of the paper, and lift the paper off. The gold stays behind on the copper.

This works whether your leaf is loose or already stuck to a backing sheet, so there is only one thing to learn.
  1. Get one piece ready, still in its paper sandwich from the cutting step.
  2. Peel off the top sheet of paper only. Go slowly and lift from one corner.

    The gold should stay lying on the bottom sheet. If it lifts with the top sheet instead, that is fine — that sheet is now your carrier, just carry on with it.

  3. Hold the paper by two opposite edges and turn it upside-down, so the gold is underneath, facing the plate.
  4. Lower it straight down onto the copper.

    Straight down, not sliding sideways. Sliding is what tears it. Once it touches, it has landed — do not try to reposition it.

  5. Rub the back of the paper with the cotton ball — small circles, light and even, for about twenty seconds.

    You are pressing the gold onto the metal through the paper. You will not see anything happen yet.

  6. Lift the paper off slowly, from one corner, keeping it low and peeling rather than pulling upward.

    The gold should stay on the copper. If it comes away with the paper, lay it back down and rub for longer before trying again.

  7. Rub the gold itself, very lightly, with the clean cotton ball.

    It will go from dull and loose-looking to bright and flat. That change is how you know it has taken.

The one thing that must not touch the gold

Nothing sticky, and no fingers, on the face that ends up pointing up. That is the surface the tip has to read. Sticky residue or skin oil on it is an insulating film, and the microscope would be trying to feel through it.

Paper is fine. Cotton is fine. Tape adhesive and fingers are not.

Tidying up before it goes anywhere near the microscope

  1. Brush the loose bits away with the soft brush, off the plate and onto the paper.

    Little torn scraps at the edges will lift later and move under the tip. Get rid of them now.

  2. Look through the magnifier. You want one region at least 2–3 mm across with no wrinkle, no crease, and no loose flap standing proud.

    A high point is what the tip meets first, and that is exactly what made the last sample useless.

Part 5Pin the edge down

Why bother. Rubbed-down gold usually holds, but "usually" is not good enough for something that must not move under the tip. A tab of copper tape over one edge does two jobs at once: it clamps the gold mechanically, and it guarantees the electrical connection even if the rubbed-down contact is poor.
  1. Cut a small tab of copper tape, a few millimetres.
  2. Decide where the tip will land — roughly the middle of your flat patch.
  3. Stick the tab over the gold at the edge furthest from that spot, so it overlaps the gold by a millimetre or two and lands on the copper beyond it.

    Keep it well clear of the imaging area. You are pinning a corner, not framing a picture.

  4. Press it down firmly and rub the tab down.

Part 6The three readings that decide whether it goes back on

Power still off. Plate still off the instrument for the first reading. Take the gold reading at a corner, gently, away from your flat patch — a meter probe will dent gold leaf.

The last two are checked after the plate is back on, and they are the same two checks that passed on 16 September. Silent on both means there is still a gap — which is what you want before you start closing it.

Stop  Stop and ask if any of these happen

Gold to sample-plate wire is silent after the tab is on. The connection is broken somewhere and guessing where will waste the session.

Gold to the tip holder beeps once the plate is refitted. That means the tip is touching the gold. Back the plate off immediately — do not power anything up.

The leaf will not lie flat after two attempts. Better to stop with a known-bad sample than to image a wrinkle for an hour.

You run out of gold leaf. Say so before ordering anything — there may be a simpler answer.

Part 7Put it back

  1. Check your photograph and match the orientation exactly.
  2. Bring the plate in straight from behind, not swinging past the tip.
  3. Rubber bands back on, same as they were.
  4. Take the last two readings from Part 6 — gold to ground, gold to tip holder. Both silent.
  5. Stop there.

    Do not wind the screws in yet. Closing the gap by hand is the next job and it has its own procedure in docs/NEXT_SESSION_PLAN.md — turn the two side-by-side screws in tiny equal amounts until the meter beeps tip-to-gold, then back both off 1/16 of a turn, which is about 20 µm.
